## A few plain-language basics first

This lesson uses some everyday terms. Here they are in plain words:

- **Offer:** the clear value you promise a client in exchange for money — the whole package of what they get, how fast, and what it costs.
- **Retainer:** a fixed monthly fee the client pays you no matter what results you get. Owen has us avoid these at the start.
- **Performance-based pricing:** you get paid only when you produce a result (a lead, an appointment, a sale), not a flat monthly fee.
- **Risk reversal:** anything that moves the risk of failure off the client and onto you — a guarantee, or "only pay when it works."
- **Tire kicker:** someone who takes up your time but was never going to seriously commit or pay.
- **Ad spend:** the money paid to Meta/Facebook to actually run the ads. Separate from your fee.

You do not need to memorise these. Each is explained again the first time it matters.

## Part 1: What an offer actually is

An offer lives between two points: **where your prospect is now** and **where they want to be.** The offer is the bridge across.

```text
CURRENT STATE                              DESIRED STATE
roofer doing $30k/mo        ── offer ──▶   booked out, crews full,
on referrals + door-knocking               predictable jobs weekly
worried every slow week
```

Your job is to make that bridge look so safe that crossing it feels like the only possible option. That is what a good offer does — it is not just a promise, it is a repositioning of the client's whole situation.

> 🔑 **The offer is the highest-leverage lever in the business. Fix the offer before you fix your sales.**

## Part 2: The diagnostic order — market, offer, persuasion

When a business is not growing, Owen checks three things **in this order**:

| # | Lever | Question | How often it's the problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Market** | Is the market viable, or dying (like newspapers)? | Rarely — most niches are fine |
| 2 | **Offer strength** | Is the promise weak or commoditized? | **Usually this one** |
| 3 | **Persuasion** | Are your sales skills the bottleneck? | Sometimes, but check the other two first |

The trap most agency owners fall into: a **normal market + a normal, copied offer**. To win from there you must become *exceptionally* good at sales to make up for two things that are merely average. Most people never do.

The beginner's cheat code: **normal market + a genuinely good offer + shaky beginner sales = success.** You do not need elite sales skills if the offer does the heavy lifting. And if you picked a small micro-niche in Lesson 4 (driveways, artificial turf, security systems), you get some of the benefit of a great market too — few competitors, faster wins.

> ✅ **What to do about it:** do not blame the market and do not rush to sales training. Pour your energy into the offer — it is the lever with the most give.

## Part 3: The six components of a winning offer

Here is a strong offer, stated the way a prospect would hear it:

> *"Pay us for every single shown appointment. No retainers or anything else — just cover ad spend. I'll generate your leads and call them for you, and you only pay me if they actually show up. I'll guarantee you six jobs in the first month, or you get the next month free."* — Owen's example

Strip it back to fundamentals and every winning offer is made of **six parts**:

| # | Component | What it is | In the example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Dream outcome** | The desired state, ideally in numbers | "Six roofing jobs" (or "$20k new monthly revenue") |
| 2 | **Timeframe** | How fast | "In the first month" |
| 3 | **Method** | The special way you get there | "I'll generate your leads and call them for you" |
| 4 | **Safety net** | Risk reversal — guarantee or pay-per-result | "Only pay if they show; six jobs or next month free" |
| 5 | **Pricing** | The model *and* the number | Performance-based, per shown appointment |
| 6 | **Qualification** | Who this is (and isn't) for | "Established roofers only" / "plumbers with 2+ trucks" |

A note on the **method**: push it toward what the client actually wants. Owen: *"These guys want appointments and jobs. They don't care as much about leads."* "I'll generate you appointments and confirm them for you" is stronger than "I'll generate you leads."

> 💡 **Guarantees vs over-promising.** Owen is honest that a guarantee can *backfire* on a pay-per-result offer: *"having a guarantee almost seems a little bit too much... it shows that you're not even confident in your services."* If you already only get paid when they show up, that IS the risk reversal — bolting a bold guarantee on top can read as desperation, or worse, become a promise you cannot keep. Use one *or* the other, and never guarantee a number you have no basis for. Inflated promises are the express lane to refunds and, under FTC rules, to legal trouble.

## Part 4: The apprentice trap

Every beginner's instinct is to charge a big upfront fee "because it sounds good." Owen calls this the apprentice trap.

> Picture a roofing crew that just hired a brand-new apprentice. First day. The owner hands the kid the keys to a **$40,000 re-roof, solo** — "Good luck, you got this." The client is paying a premium. The kid messes it up, it leaks in the first storm, the client demands a refund. The company eats the loss and the apprentice is done before he ever got started.

**You are the apprentice.** Charge $4–5k upfront on day one and here is what actually happens:

- ❌ **Refunds and chargebacks.** You fumble one onboarding step (because you *are* inexperienced), the client decides you have no clue, and you get a chargeback before you've even run an ad.
- ❌ **You get caught in a lie.** To justify a big number with no track record, you inflate it with fake case studies and promises you can't keep. "It works for a month. It does. But it doesn't end well."
- ❌ **No skin in the game.** Their cash is already in your account, so the desperate pressure to deliver is gone — and results suffer.

> 🔑 **You are not in your earning season yet. You are in your learning season.** Your offer should line up with that: you only win when the client wins.

## Part 5: Skin in the game and the small setup fee

If you're not taking a big upfront fee, are you working for free until results land? No. Owen recommends a **small setup fee — anywhere from the low hundreds up to about $1,500.**

Why it matters:

- Gets rid of tire kickers (companies that won't pay *anything* upfront usually never commit).
- Gets the client invested enough to take you seriously and actually follow up on the leads you send.
- Gets their **card on file** for the pay-per billing that follows.
- Lets *you* practice selling without a terrifying $5k price tag on the call.

Owen's warning from experience: *"If it's free, they won't take you seriously and they'll ghost you. That's happened to me literally like five times when I started."*

> 💡 **Alternatives for taking that setup fee:** Owen uses **Stripe**. Real substitutes include Square, PayPal, Braintree, and Paddle — pick one and open the account **in your own legal name** (using anyone else's identity is fraud, full stop).

## Part 6: The performance ladder

Performance-based pricing has five rungs. As you climb, the service feels more valuable and the client is more accountable. The dollar figures below are Owen's **illustrative** roofing numbers (per result, ad spend already subtracted) — recompute them for your own niche.

```text
RUNG                        WHAT TRIGGERS PAYMENT           ~ROOFING $   NOTES
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1  Pay per lead             a lead comes in                    $14       weak, poor retention
2  Pay per qualified lead   lead fits budget/location/ready    $58       fine, barely better than 3
3  Pay per appointment      a meeting lands on the calendar    $88       decent
4  Pay per SHOWN appt.      they actually show up              $147   ◀── the sweet spot
5  Pay per closed deal      the client makes money             ~$490     max alignment
```

- **Pay per lead** is perceived as low-value — the client sees every tire kicker and feels ripped off. Bad for retention.
- **Pay per qualified lead** is fine, but so close to an appointment that you might as well climb one rung.
- **Pay per shown appointment** is Owen's pick: *"This, in my opinion, is the sweet spot... if you want to make the most money and have the most accountability from your clients, then payer shown appointment is the best way to do that."*
- **Pay per closed deal** is maximum alignment — you only eat when they eat — but it depends on your service and market.

**Handling no-shows on pay-per-shown:** you bill every appointment on the calendar; for the ~15% who don't show, you simply credit the client on a future appointment. You confirm what happened with an automatic form after each appointment.

> ⚠️ **Reality check — the "$900 if they lie" clause.** Owen prevents clients from lying on that form by writing into the agreement that a false report triggers a **$900 charge**. Be careful here. A penalty clause like this may be **unenforceable** in many places (courts often void charges that look punitive rather than tied to real damages), and trying to collect it from a local business owner can torch your reputation in a small niche where word travels. A cleaner path: keep the after-appointment form, cross-check against your ad platform's conversation logs, and address disputes as a conversation — not a fine.

> 💡 **Why beginners should genuinely prefer performance pricing — beyond the tactics.** It is more *honest*. You're not faking being a giant agency; you're "a smart solo business owner confident enough to bet on yourself." When your pay is tied to a result, your incentives and the client's are actually aligned — you have to make it work. That authenticity closes deals *and* it's the right thing to sell.

## Part 7: Build the product around the offer

> "Never build your offer around your product as it is. Instead, build your product around your offer." — Owen

Most people cap their promise at what they already know how to do ("I can only get this guy one or two jobs a month"). Don't. Start with a **bold claim that scares you a little** — then build the product that backs it up. Your skills will rise to meet the promise.

> 💡 **The honest boundary on "a claim that scares you."** "Bold enough to make you uncomfortable" is good marketing psychology. "A number you have no path to and no intention of honoring" is a lie that ends in refunds and FTC exposure. Bold and *reachable-if-you-hustle* is the target — not fabricated.

## Key takeaways

1. **The offer is the highest-leverage lever.** Weak sales is usually a weak-offer symptom.
2. **Diagnose in order:** market → offer → persuasion. It's almost always the offer.
3. **Six components:** dream outcome, timeframe, method, safety net, pricing, qualification.
4. **Don't take the apprentice's premium job.** Big upfront fees on day one cause refunds, lies, and dead deals.
5. **Small setup fee ($low-hundreds to ~$1,500) + performance pay.** Skin in the game, but you win only when the client wins.
6. **Pay per shown appointment is the sweet spot.** Best money and accountability for most niches.

## Common pitfalls

- ❌ **Blaming your sales skills for a weak offer.** Fix the offer first; no sales training rescues a bad one.
- ❌ **Charging $4–5k upfront with no track record.** This is the apprentice trap — expect chargebacks and a ruined first impression.
- ❌ **Stacking a bold guarantee onto a pay-per offer.** It can read as low confidence — the pay-per structure is already your risk reversal.
- ❌ **Writing coercive penalty clauses (like $900 for a false report).** Often unenforceable and reputation-wrecking in a small niche.
- ❌ **Charging free to "win" a client.** They won't take you seriously and will ghost. Always get a small commitment.

## 🛠️ Capstone Project: Draft your V1 winning offer

> This is the main hands-on project for the lesson. You will end with a written offer you could actually pitch this week — imperfect on purpose. Ship it, then refine it.

### What you will build

A one-page "winning offer" for the micro-niche you chose in Lesson 4, containing all six components in plain client-facing language, plus a chosen rung on the performance ladder and a setup-fee number. This becomes the offer your later modules (ads, outreach, sales) are all built to deliver.

### Why this is the perfect practice

| Lesson idea | Where you use it in your V1 offer |
|---|---|
| Six components | You fill in all six, one line each |
| Diagnostic order | You confirm your niche is a viable market |
| Performance ladder | You pick one rung and justify it |
| Setup fee / skin in the game | You set a number from low-hundreds to ~$1,500 |
| Build product around offer | You write a claim that scares you a little |

### Milestones (build them in order, each one works on its own)

1. **Name the customer and dream outcome.** Your micro-niche + the outcome they want, in numbers ("six new roofing jobs a month"). Note what that outcome is worth to them in dollars.
2. **Set the method and timeframe.** Done-for-you or done-with-you? What you actually do (run ads → generate appointments → confirm them), and by when.
3. **Choose your safety net.** A pay-per-result structure OR a guarantee — not both. Write the exact risk-reversal sentence.
4. **Pick a rung on the ladder.** Default to pay per shown appointment unless you have a reason not to. Write down why.
5. **Set the setup fee.** One number, low-hundreds to ~$1,500. Write the one line you'll say on a call to justify it.
6. **Write the qualification.** Who it's for ("established roofers only," "plumbers with 2+ trucks running").
7. **Assemble the full offer.** Paste all six into one clean paragraph, phrased the way the prospect hears it. Optionally paste your notes into Claude/ChatGPT: *"Help me tighten this V1 offer for [niche] into one clear, honest, performance-based pitch."*
8. **Stretch goals.** Draft a second variant one rung up (pay per closed deal). Write the exact no-show credit policy you'll use.

### How you will know you are done

- ✅ All six components are written and each is one clear sentence.
- ✅ You've chosen a specific pricing rung and can say why in one line.
- ✅ Your setup fee is a real number you'd say out loud.
- ✅ The dream-outcome claim makes you a little uncomfortable — but you could honestly deliver it if you hustle.

> 💡 **Keep yourself honest:** if your guarantee is a number you can't back up, cut it. An honest pay-per offer beats a bold lie every time.

## Practice exercises (optional extra reps)

> **What these are:** small, self-contained tasks, each giving focused practice on one idea. Optional and independent; the Capstone already touches all of them.

### Exercise 1: Label the components (foundational)
Take Owen's example offer and label each clause with which of the six components it is. Where is the timeframe hiding? Which component is weakest?

### Exercise 2: Ladder swap (intermediate)
Rewrite your safety-net sentence three ways — as pay-per-lead, pay-per-shown-appointment, and pay-per-closed-deal. Note how the client's perceived value and your accountability change at each rung.

### Exercise 3: Rescue a bad offer (advanced)
Write a deliberately weak, commoditized offer ("$1,500/month retainer, I'll manage your Facebook ads"). Then rebuild it into a performance-based offer with all six components. Explain in two lines why the rebuilt version is both more persuasive and more honest.
