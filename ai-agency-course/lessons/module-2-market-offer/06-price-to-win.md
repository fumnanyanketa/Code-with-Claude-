## In one sentence

You set your price by working backwards from the money your client makes — take the profit on one job, keep the client at a 5x return floor, subtract what the ads cost, and whatever is left is the most you can charge — then you word the offer around the result, not the mechanism.

> 🎯 **Where this lesson is heading.** It builds to a hands-on **Capstone
> Project** where you produce a finished, one-page pricing sheet for your niche —
> an all-in per-shown price plus a setup fee, with the 5x math shown on the page.
> Everything before the Capstone teaches the four moves that get you there. If you
> want to see the finish line first, jump to the **"Capstone Project"** section,

## A few plain-language basics first

This lesson uses some everyday business terms. Here they are in plain words:

- **Margin (profit margin):** the slice of a sale that is actually profit after
  costs. A $14,000 roof at a 35% margin leaves the roofer about $4,900 in profit.
- **ROI (return on investment):** how many dollars come back for each dollar spent.
  "5x ROI" means every $1 spent returns $5.
- **Ad spend:** the money paid to Meta (Facebook/Instagram) to run the ads. It is
  a separate cost from your fee — someone has to pay it, and deciding who is a big
  part of this lesson.
- **Pay-per-shown (per shown appointment):** you get paid only when a booked
  prospect actually shows up to the meeting. One of five pricing models on the
  "performance ladder" you met in Lesson 5.
- **Setup fee:** a small one-time charge (low hundreds up to ~$1,500) the client
  pays to get started. It filters out non-serious buyers and gets a card on file.
- **Churn / retention:** churn is the share of clients who leave in a month;
  retention is the share who stay. Low retention means you are always hunting new
  clients.

You do not need to memorise these. Each is re-explained the first time it matters.

## Why this lesson matters

In Lesson 5 you learned what an offer is made of. This lesson answers the question
that terrifies every beginner: *what number do I say on the call?* Guessing gets you
two bad outcomes — price too high and you get refunds and chargebacks you cannot
survive; price too low and your client stops valuing you and cancels. Owen's fix is
to stop guessing entirely. "We're going to reverse engineer from a client's ROI,"
he says. The price is not a feeling; it is the output of a short calculation anchored
to how much money you make your client. Get this right and, in Owen's words, you are
"selling dollars at a discount" — you make it "stupid for someone to say no."

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. Reverse-engineer a per-job price from your client's profit using the 5x ROI floor.
2. Convert that per-job ceiling into a billing chart across all five performance models.
3. Decide whether you or the client pays ad spend, based on your cash reserves.
4. Word an offer around the result (the "palm trees") instead of the mechanism.
5. Explain why raising price can raise perceived value — and when that is a trap.
6. Use an AI assistant to run the pricing math, and sanity-check its numbers yourself.

## Part 1: The 5x floor — the number that decides who stays

Before any arithmetic, one rule sets the whole thing: **your client should make at
least a 5x return on every dollar they spend** — on the ads and on you combined.

> "The floor is your client making at least a 5x return on every dollar they spend
> on this... If it's above 5x, they stay happy. If it's below 5x, they cancel."
> *— Owen*

That single number sorts your client into one of three zones:

| Zone | Return | What actually happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Meh** | 0–5x | One bad week from cancelling. No review. No referral. |
| **Solid** | 5–10x | Happy, pays on time, will give you a testimonial. |
| **Nuts** | 10x+ | Refers everyone, "does the marketing for you." |

Aim for the **solid zone**, and never let the client dip below **5x**. Five is the
benchmark you build the price around. Everything in Part 2 is just enforcing this
floor with math.

> 🔑 **The 5x floor is the whole pricing philosophy in one number: charge whatever
> keeps the client above a 5x return, and not a dollar more.**

## Part 2: The pricing math, one step at a time (roofing worked example)

Owen walks this with real roofing numbers. Follow each line — it is only five steps.

**Step 1 — Average revenue per closed job.** What is one job worth? For roofing,
about **$14,000** per re-roof. (Look yours up on Google or ask an AI; Owen even
suggests calling businesses in your niche: *"Hey, I'm doing a school project..."*
and asking what jobs they want and profit most from.)

**Step 2 — Profit per closed job.** Multiply revenue by the margin. Roofing runs a
**~35%** margin, so:

```text
$14,000  ×  0.35  =  ~$4,900 profit per closed job
```

**Step 3 — What the client can justify spending (apply the 5x floor).** Divide the
profit by 5. This is the *most* a client can spend to win one job and still clear a
5x return:

```text
$4,900  ÷  5  =  ~$980 the client can justify per job
```

That $980 is the pie. It gets split between **the ads** and **you**. Owen: "This is
your room for margin. This is where you can get paid for every single job you get them."

**Step 4 — Subtract the ad cost per closed deal.** For roofing, it costs Meta roughly
**$500** in ad spend to produce one closed job (Owen quotes $500–$650; Part 4 shows
how to derive it for any niche). Take it out of the pie:

```text
$980  −  $500  =  ~$480  →  rounds to ~$490 max agency fee per closed deal
```

**Step 5 — That $480–$490 is your ceiling per closed deal.** It is the most you can
charge, per job, and still keep the client above 5x. Now turn it into a billing chart
for whichever model on the ladder you sell. Each row is derived by walking the funnel
backwards (a shown appointment closes ~30% of the time, so per-shown ≈ 30% of the
per-close number, and so on):

| You charge... | Per what | Roofing price (agency fee only, ads excluded) |
|---|---|---|
| Pay-per-close | closed deal | **~$500** |
| **Pay-per-shown** | shown appointment | **~$147** ← the sweet spot |
| Pay-per-appointment | booked meeting | ~$88 |
| Pay-per-qualified-lead | qualified lead | ~$58 |
| Pay-per-lead | raw lead | ~$14 |

> 💡 **Every number in that chart is the fee that goes to *you* — ad spend was already
> subtracted in Step 4.** Do not double-count it.

Owen's recommendation from Lesson 5 stands: **pay-per-shown** is the sweet spot —
enough accountability that the client takes it seriously, without waiting for a slow
sales cycle to close. For roofing that lands at **~$147 per shown appointment**.

> ✅ **What to do about it:** run these five steps for *your* niche now, on paper. You
> will formalise it in the Capstone, but doing it once by hand is how the logic sticks.

## Part 3: Who pays ad spend — and why the math has no winner

Here is the twist that confuses most people. Whether **the client pays ad spend** or
**you pay ad spend**, three things come out identical:

1. **What the client pays each month** (roughly the same total).
2. **The client's ROI** (same spend, same return — about 5.5x in Owen's real example).
3. **Your net profit per client** (about the same either way).

Owen's real client spends **$1,800/month** on ads, which produces about **12 shown
appointments**, closing ~30% into **4 jobs/month** at $14k each — around **$19,600/month
profit** for the roofer. Run it both ways:

| | Client pays ad spend | You pay ad spend |
|---|---|---|
| Your per-shown fee | ~$147 | ~$297 (ads folded in) |
| Ad budget on your books | $0 (their card) | $1,800 |
| Client's total monthly cost | ~$3,600 | ~$3,600 |
| Client's ROI | ~5.5x | ~5.5x |
| **Your net per client** | ~$1,700 | ~$1,700 |

> 🔑 **The ads are still the ads. Moving who swipes the card does not change the client's
> cost, the client's ROI, or your net. What it changes is cash flow, positioning, and
> sales resistance.**

So how do you choose? By your **reserves** — how much working capital you can safely
tie up in a client's ads before the fee catches up:

- **Client pays ad spend** — best when you have **under ~$5k** in reserves. No capital
  needed to onboard, clean books, less perceived risk for you. Downside: *more* sales
  resistance, because on the call it *sounds* like three separate costs ("$500 setup,
  $147 per appointment, *and* ad spend"), even though the math is the same.
- **You pay ad spend** — best when you have **~$5k+** in reserves. One clean number
  ("$297 per shown appointment, that's it"), far less sales resistance, faster
  onboarding (you just duplicate the campaign on your own card), and you can scale a
  client's budget without asking permission. Downside: you carry the cash-flow risk and
  float the ad money for a few days before the fees turn you profitable.

Owen's own lean: if you *have* the funds, **pay ad spend** — lower resistance means a
lower cost to acquire each client. If you don't, **let the client pay** and consider
shaving your fee to offset the extra resistance.

> ⚠️ **Reality check — the "put ad spend on your AMEX for the points" idea.** Owen notes
> that paying ad spend yourself racks up credit-card travel points ("you'll never have to
> pay for a flight again"). That is real, and fine — but the points are a *side effect*,
> not a reason. When you float thousands in ad spend on a card, **you** carry the cash-flow
> risk if a client pays late or churns. Chase results, not points, and never spend ad money
> you cannot afford to be out for a few weeks.

## Part 4: Deriving the ad cost for *any* niche

Roofing's ~$500-per-job ad cost isn't magic — you can compute it for your niche from
two numbers you can look up or estimate: **cost per appointment** and **show rate**.

```text
cost per appointment  ÷  show rate           =  cost per SHOWN appointment
cost per shown appointment  ×  3  (a ~30% close rate)  =  ad cost per CLOSED job
```

Worked for roofing: `$90 ÷ 60% = $150 per shown`, then `$150 × 3 ≈ $450` to close a job
(Owen pessimistically rounds to $500–$650). A couple more, illustrative:

| Niche | Cost/appt | Show rate | Cost/shown | Cost/job (~×3) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roofing | ~$90 | ~60% | ~$150 | ~$450 |
| Painting | ~$70 | ~60% | ~$117 | ~$340 |
| Bathroom remodel | ~$120 | ~55% | ~$218 | ~$650 |

> 💡 **These are Owen's estimates, not gospel** — he's "not in all these niches." After
> ~30 days of live campaigns, **replace them with your real data** and recompute. Your ads,
> funnel, and lead-conversion setup swing these numbers hard (a lead form vs. a landing page
> alone changes cost per appointment a lot).

## Part 5: Word the offer around the result, not the mechanism

You now have the numbers. But Owen is blunt: "How people perceive your offer is more
important than how valuable it actually is when it comes to sales." Same offer, different
words, opposite outcome.

> "Nobody books a flight for the flight. They book it for the palm trees." *— Owen*

So **lead with the result, never the mechanism.** Nobody buys "we'll run your Facebook
ads" or "we'll implement an AI bot." They buy **jobs**. Sell the palm trees.

There is a ladder of how *arguable* each deliverable is — climb it as high as you can:

```text
raw lead          "just a name and a number"       → easy to dismiss
qualified lead    "warmer, but still iffy"          → arguable
booked appointment "real, someone on the calendar"  → harder to argue
shown appointment  "they actually turned up"        → strong
CLOSED JOB         "exactly what they want"         → impossible to argue with
```

Frame the promise as close to **closed jobs** as your model honestly allows: *"We'll get
you six to eight jobs on a pay-per-result basis"* beats *"We'll get you 50 leads in 90
days"* every time — even if the underlying work is identical.

Two more wording levers:

- **Safety net.** Pay-per-results *is* your guarantee. Owen warns that bolting a separate
  "you can get a refund" promise on top can backfire — it signals you don't trust your own
  model. Let the pricing structure carry the safety.
- **Effort & sacrifice.** The less the client has to do, the more valuable it feels. You run
  the ads, you (or AI) call the leads, automation runs the system. Lowering their effort
  raises perceived value without touching the price.

## Part 6: Price-to-value, the point of cancellation, and the cycle of price

Why does anyone buy? "Purchasing decisions are made solely because the value is superior to
price." And they keep buying **until value dips to or below price** — that crossing point is
the **point of cancellation**.

```text
     value  ─────────────╮
                          ╰──── point of cancellation ───  value ≤ price → they leave
     price  ──────────────────────────────────────────
```

Most agencies live *below* that line — which is why they churn clients and must forever hunt
new ones. Owen ties it to metrics: if your churn rate outruns your retention and referral
rates, you are stuck on the treadmill. His two options when value slips below price:

1. Lower your price → and slowly fail.
2. **Raise your value** → and succeed.

You can only cut price down to zero, but you can raise value to infinity. This leads to the
counter-intuitive **cycle of price**: *raising* your price can *raise* perceived value.

> "They had three wines... one was a $1 wine, one was $100, one was $1,000. Every single
> person said the $1,000 wine was the best wine even though they were all the same." *— Owen*

A higher price forces you to deliver a premium service, which justifies the price, which lets
you reinvest in better systems and results — the opposite of the race-to-the-bottom where you
copy a competitor, undercut them slightly, and commoditise yourself.

> ⚠️ **Reality check — the wine study is real, but it is not a licence to overcharge.** Price
> anchoring genuinely lifts *perceived* value. It does **not** lift *delivered* value. If you
> raise price and the results don't follow, you have just pushed yourself faster toward the
> point of cancellation — and a bad local reputation. Raise price *and* back it with real
> delivery, or don't raise it.

## Part 7: Let an AI run the math — then check it

The whole pricing calculation is exactly the kind of arithmetic an AI assistant does well.
Owen's repeated advice: screenshot the niche chart or upload the worksheet as a PDF and prompt:

> *"I want to do [niche]. Given these averages, what are my best prices to be as profitable as
> possible? Give me a range, and keep my client above a 5x return."*

> 💡 **Tool alternatives.** Owen says Claude; **ChatGPT and Gemini do this equally well** —
> use whichever you have. All three handle the math and the PDF upload.

> ❌ **Do not paste the AI's numbers straight onto your pricing sheet.** LLMs make arithmetic
> and unit slips (mixing per-shown with per-close, dropping the ad-cost subtraction, using a
> stale margin). **Re-run the five steps by hand once** and confirm the AI's final fee matches
> yours before you commit. The AI is a calculator, not the authority.

## Key takeaways

1. **Price backwards from client ROI, not from a feeling.** Profit → ÷5 → minus ad cost =
   your ceiling. For roofing: $4,900 → $980 → −$500 → ~$480/close.
2. **The 5x floor is the whole rule.** Above 5x they stay and refer; below it they cancel.
3. **Turn the per-close ceiling into a billing chart.** Pay-per-shown (~$147 for roofing) is
   the sweet spot on the ladder.
4. **Who pays ad spend doesn't change client cost, client ROI, or your net** — only cash flow,
   positioning, and sales resistance. Choose by your reserves (~$5k line).
5. **Sell the palm trees.** Lead with closed jobs, never "we run Facebook ads."
6. **Raising price can raise perceived value — only if delivery follows.** Otherwise you speed
   to the point of cancellation.

## Common pitfalls

- ❌ **Charging a big upfront fee as a beginner.** It invites refunds and chargebacks before
   you've run a single ad, and removes your pressure to deliver. Use a small setup fee
   (low hundreds to ~$1,500) plus pay-per-results instead.
- ❌ **Double-counting ad spend.** The billing-chart fees already exclude ads. Don't subtract
   it twice or add it back into the fee by accident.
- ❌ **Pricing below the 5x floor to "win the deal."** You'll churn the client in weeks — more
   expensive than never signing them. Raise value, don't cut price.
- ❌ **Selling the mechanism.** "We'll run your ads / build an AI bot" doesn't close. "We'll get
   you six to eight jobs" does.
- ❌ **Trusting the AI's arithmetic blindly.** Sanity-check every figure by hand once.
- ❌ **Racing competitors to the bottom.** Copy-and-undercut commoditises you. Charge a premium
   and deliver a premium.

## 🛠️ Capstone Project: your niche's one-page pricing sheet

> This is the main hands-on project for the lesson. You will turn everything above into a single
> page you can actually quote from on a sales call — kept small on purpose so you finish it today.

### What you will build

A one-page **pricing sheet** for your chosen niche containing: an **all-in per-shown price**, a
**setup fee**, and the **5x check** written out so anyone reading it can verify the client clears a
5x return. It is the pricing component of your agency — the thing you'll read off when a prospect
asks "so how much is this?"

Its pieces map straight to the lesson:

- The five-step math (Part 2) → the derivation shown on the page.
- The ad-cost formula (Part 4) → your niche's cost-per-job.
- The ad-spend decision (Part 3) → whether the per-shown price is the ~$147 or ~$297 style.
- Result-first wording (Part 5) → the one-line offer at the top.

### Why this is the perfect practice

| Lesson idea | Where you use it on the sheet |
|---|---|
| 5x floor | The "5x check" line that proves the client's return |
| Five-step pricing math | The derivation block (profit → ÷5 → −ad cost) |
| Ad-cost-per-niche formula | Your cost-per-shown and cost-per-job numbers |
| Who-pays-ad-spend decision | Whether your per-shown price folds ads in |
| Result-first wording | The headline offer line |

### Milestones (build them in order, each one works on its own)

1. **Pick the niche and pull two numbers.** Average revenue per closed job, and profit margin
   (Google them or ask an AI). Write both at the top. Smallest version: two numbers on a line.
2. **Run steps 1–3.** revenue × margin = profit; profit ÷ 5 = what the client can justify. Show
   both results.
3. **Derive your ad cost per job.** Use `cost-per-appointment ÷ show-rate × 3`. Write the
   cost-per-shown and the cost-per-job.
4. **Compute your per-close ceiling and the billing chart.** Justifiable − ad cost = max fee per
   close; convert to a per-shown fee.
5. **Choose who pays ad spend.** State your reserves, pick client-pays (~$147-style) or you-pay
   (~$297-style, ads folded in), and set your all-in per-shown price accordingly.
6. **Add the setup fee and the headline.** Pick a setup fee (low hundreds to ~$1,500). Write the
   one-line, result-first offer across the top (e.g. "Six+ roofing jobs on a pay-per-result basis").
7. **Write the 5x check.** In one line, show total client monthly cost vs. monthly profit and the
   resulting multiple — prove it's ≥5x.
8. **Stretch goals.** Add a you-pay *and* a client-pays variant side by side; add a second niche;
   have an AI critique your sheet, then correct its math.

### How you will know you are done

- ✅ The page states an all-in per-shown price and a setup fee.
- ✅ The five-step derivation is visible, not just the final number.
- ✅ The 5x check shows the client clears at least a 5x return.
- ✅ The ad-spend decision is stated with the reason (your reserves).
- ✅ You re-checked every figure by hand, not just trusted the AI.

> 💡 **Keep yourself honest:** if your 5x check comes out *below* 5x, the sheet isn't done — lower
> your fee or raise the value until it clears. Do not ship a sheet that quietly churns the client.

## Practice exercises (optional extra reps)

> **What these are:** small, self-contained tasks, each giving focused practice on one idea.

### Exercise 1: The five-step drill (foundational)
Take painting ($3,400-ish per job, look up the margin) and run the five steps to a per-shown fee.

### Exercise 2: Same offer, both ad-spend models (intermediate)

Invent an agency priced *below* the point of cancellation (value < price). Fix it two ways — once by
cutting price, once by raising value — and argue which client relationship survives longer, and why.
