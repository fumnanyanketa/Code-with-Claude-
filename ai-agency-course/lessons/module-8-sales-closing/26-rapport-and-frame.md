## In one sentence

The closing call has five stages — **Rapport → Frame → Discovery → Pitch → Close** — and this lesson teaches the first two: open by reading which of four "energy types" your prospect is, "chameleon" your delivery to match, generate energy when they show up flat, and then take gentle control of the call by setting its agenda.

> 🎯 **Where this lesson is heading.** It builds to a hands-on **Capstone Project** where you write your own PACE-adaptive rapport-and-frame opening for your niche, plus a personal rule for handling disrespect, and rehearse it in three mock openings. Everything before the Capstone teaches the skills you will use there. If you want to see the finish line first, jump to the **"Capstone Project"** section, then come back.

## A few plain-language basics first

This lesson uses some everyday sales terms. Here they are in plain words:

- **Closing call:** the main sales call (often on Zoom) where you actually try to sign the client. It comes *after* a shorter "setting" call that booked it (Module 7).
- **Rapport:** the friendly first minutes where the prospect decides they like you and want to keep talking.
- **Frame:** who is in charge of the conversation. The person asking the questions and setting the agenda holds the frame.
- **PACE:** Owen's label for four energy types — **P**ractical, **A**ction, **S**ocial, **E**motional. A quick read on who you're talking to.
- **Chameleon:** adjusting your own delivery (word choice, pace, humor) to match the prospect's energy.
- **Mini-discovery:** a couple of quick qualifying questions early, used only when a prospect seems like they might be a waste of your time.

You do not need to memorise these. Each is explained again the first time it matters.

## Why this lesson matters

Owen says experienced reps "can look at a call and watch the first five minutes and basically tell whether the guy's going to close." The opening is not small talk you rush through to get to the "real" call — it *is* part of the real call. Get the first few minutes right and the prospect opens up, trusts you, and lets you lead. Get them wrong and you spend the next 40 minutes fighting to recover. This lesson gives you a repeatable way to open strong and take the lead without being pushy.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. Recite the five-stage master framework and explain why Owen wants your first ~30 calls scriptless.
2. Run the three-step rapport process: friendly intro, chameleon-match, cliché question.
3. Read a prospect's PACE energy type from early keywords and vibe.
4. Generate energy for a flat prospect instead of mirroring their low energy — without forcing fake "bro energy."
5. Run a mini-discovery when a prospect seems unqualified, and reschedule respectfully when they show up disrespectful or unprepared.
6. Take control by setting the call's agenda ("frame"), adapting the tone to the energy type.

## Part 1: The master framework, and why you go scriptless first

Every closing call, Owen says, runs through the same five stages:

```text
RAPPORT  →  FRAME  →  DISCOVERY  →  PITCH  →  CLOSE
(1-4 min)   (1-3 min)  (15-35 min)   (1-4 min)  (moments)
```

> "Rapport, frame, discovery, pitch, close. This is the master sales framework. I know it sounds silly and it's very short — it's literally five words — but this is all you need." *— Owen*

This lesson covers the first two stages. Discovery (where the sale is actually made) is Lesson 27; pitch and close come after.

Before any of it, Owen gives one blunt instruction:

> "I need you to take your first 30 sales calls without a script. And I know this is going to sound insane… but I promise if you do this properly, you will 5x your progress." *— Owen*

The reasoning: a script keeps your head buried in the next line instead of *present* with the person in front of you. You learn to sell by talking to people and reacting to what they actually say — not by reading. Start scriptless, and later (Lesson 27's capstone) you'll build a loose *framework* to lean on, never a word-for-word script.

> 🔑 **The whole call is five stages. Rapport and frame are the on-ramp — short, but they decide whether the prospect lets you drive.**

> ⚠️ **Reality check.** "Take 30 calls with no script" is fine for *practice* calls and mock calls. But every one of those calls is a real person's time and money on the line. Going scriptless is not a licence to wing it or make claims you can't back up. Be prepared, be honest about what you can deliver, and don't treat live prospects as disposable reps.

## Part 2: The three-step rapport process

Rapport is the first one to four minutes: introduce yourself, ask how they're doing, be genuinely friendly. Owen's whole instruction for mastering it is disarmingly simple — "just be friendly… smile. Go into it with the frame of mind that you can actually change this person's life, because you can." There's a three-step structure underneath:

1. **Friendly, exciting introduction.** Smile (they can hear it), make it warm, act like you *want* to be there — because you should. Compare the two openings Owen contrasts: a flat "Hey. Good. Nice to put a name to the face. Where are you calling out of?" versus a bright, curious "Hey John, how are you doing today? Awesome — nice to finally put a name to the face. Where are you calling out of? *No way*, how is it there right now?" Same words, completely different outcome. Tone is the difference.
2. **Chameleon — match their personality.** You'll pick up hints of who they are from keywords, jokes, stories, even swearing. Match their language and pace so they feel they're talking to *their kind of person*.
3. **A cliché question, then share something about yourself.** "Where are you calling out of?" or "Before I get started, just so I can best help you out — what are you looking to get out of a call like this?" It gives them the illusion of control and opens a clean segue into the frame.

> ✅ **What to do about it:** don't overthink the words. Show up warm and curious, mirror their vibe, ask one easy question, and move on. Rapport is the appetiser, not the meal.

## Part 3: PACE — reading the energy type

Early in the call you can get a good read on which of four energies dominates a person. This is Owen's **PACE**:

| Type | Think of… | Wants | How to speak to them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P**ractical | Elon Musk | Logic, numbers, systems | Be efficient, tactical, process-focused |
| **A**ction | Andy Elliott | A clear-cut path, no fluff | Be decisive; "here's exactly how this works" |
| **S**ocial | Dwayne Johnson | Connection, chatting | Keep it light; be ready to cut tangents |
| **E**motional | Emma Watson | To be heard, validated | Slow down, listen, reflect feelings |

**Most B2B business owners are P or A** — practical and action-based. They want results and operations, not a heart-to-heart. You'll rarely close a purely social or emotional business owner, though they exist. So most of Owen's tactics are tuned for P/A.

> 💡 **Nuance:** everyone has all four in some ratio; there's one dominant. And Owen is clear that PACE "is not required to close deals — you could never hear of this concept and have a 40% close rate." It's a helper, not a requirement. Treat it as a hint, and never let it harden into a stereotype: "if you make an assumption that someone won't be a good fit just because of their age or gender… you might just lose the sale because of how you run the rest of the call." Read the person in front of you, not the label.

## Part 4: Generate energy, don't just mirror it

Chameleoning has one big exception. If the prospect shows up *low* energy — a flat "uh… hello" — you do **not** match it. You lift them into your energy instead.

> "The power plant doesn't have energy — it generates energy. So you have to generate the energy in the other prospect." *— Owen*

Rapport is also about **awareness**: noticing their world and using it. A Detroit Lions flag behind them? "Oh, big Lions fan, eh?" It says *we're two humans here*, and it warms the room.

One caution on chameleoning: **bro energy**. Some P/A owners will open with "What's up, bro?" — and if *they* do it, play into it. But don't initiate it:

> "Unless they're doing it, don't do it. Don't be like 'What's good, bro?' to a 50-year-old businessman that's like 'Hi, it's nice to meet you.'" *— Owen*

> ❌ **Pitfall — too much rapport.** Owen's own early mistake: "I was way too rapport heavy… I'd do rapport for 8 to 15 minutes and then I had no frame on the call." Overbuild rapport and you become their friend, lose control, and can't push later. Keep it to a few minutes.

## Part 5: Mini-discovery, and never accepting disrespect

Sometimes the opening tells you this person might be a waste of an hour — "I just want to hear the price," showing up late, smoking, on their phone, talking to someone else in the room. When you sense they're **unqualified**, skip the friendly build and go stronger: run a **mini-discovery** — a couple of quick questions (what they make, whether they're actually ready to focus) to decide if the call is even worth continuing before you invest in setting frame.

Owen is equally firm about **disrespect**. You should never just absorb it:

- Camera off, mic broken, showing up late, fixing things, lighting a cigarette? Do something to reset control — even a simple "Give me one second, I'm going to grab a water."
- Escalate honestly if it continues: *"Respectfully, if you don't think this is worth your time, we'll need to rebook — I need your full focus to see if this can actually help you."*
- If they didn't do the pre-call homework (e.g. a video they were asked to watch), **reschedule.** "If they didn't prepare, don't accept it. Reschedule the call till they are prepared."

> 💡 **This is the healthy version of "frame."** Refusing to be treated as disposable is self-respect and good filtering — bad-fit clients churn and nag. Note the honest line, though: rescheduling because someone is genuinely distracted is legitimate; using "you're not focused" as a manufactured guilt-trip on someone who *is* engaged is manipulation. Use it to protect your time, not to corner people.

## Part 6: Frame — taking control of the call

Frame is the next couple of minutes, right after rapport. You state what's going to happen on the call — the agenda — so the prospect sees you leading it.

> "Whoever's asking the questions leads the call." *— Owen*
>
> "You are either holding a frame or you're in someone else's. So you must hold your own." *— Owen*

Why it matters most for beginners: if the prospect sees you as an authority worth learning from, they'll go with you; if they see "some kid that doesn't know anything," they never will. Very experienced closers sometimes skip an explicit frame because they hold control naturally — but until you're there, say it out loud.

**Older owners with big egos** ("You're a kid, what do you know? I've been in business 45 years") are common with cold traffic. Owen's advice: don't be phased, lighten the mood, crack a joke, have charisma. "At the end of the day, sales is quite literally just a game" — most of them are challenging you for no real reason. Play back into it and hold your authority calmly.

> ⚠️ **Reality check — where "frame" curdles into steamrolling.** Setting an agenda and redirecting tangents is legitimate leadership; a prospect *wants* a competent guide. But "holding frame" tips into steamrolling when you talk over genuine questions, dismiss real objections as "losing frame," or make someone feel small for pushing back. The test: are you leading them toward a decision that's good *for them*, or just refusing to let them think? Respect the prospect. Pressure-selling wins the call and loses the client — it wrecks retention and your local reputation.

## Part 7: Five example frame sets by energy type

Owen gives five illustrative frame sets, tuned to the PACE types. Read them to *feel the differences* — then throw them away.

> ❌ **Do NOT use these as scripts.** Owen is explicit: "These are examples. Do not write them down. Do not copy them and read them out in a sales call. We do not use scripts in this program." They exist only to show how the *tone* shifts by energy type.

| Set | For | Flavour (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|
| **General** | anyone | "Appreciate you hopping on. I've got about 45 minutes — here's how this works: I'll ask a few questions to understand where you're at, and if I think we can help, I'll walk you through what we do. Fair enough?" |
| **Practical** | P types | Leans on being *tactical* and process-based: "This call is really about understanding your current process, where you're trying to go, and whether it fits what we do. I'll give you the logic and clarity to make an informed decision." |
| **Action** | A types | Leans on *no hypotheticals, clear path*: "All our work is customized, so instead of throwing hypothetical numbers at you, I just want clarity on what's really going on. We only work with qualified people — if it's a fit, great; if not, we part as friends." |
| **Social** | S types | Leans on *chatting, low pressure*: "This isn't going to be one of those stiff calls — it's a chance to get a feel for each other and see if it makes sense to explore anything further." |
| **Emotional** | E types | Leans on *coaching and clarity*: "What I really try to do is help you understand where you are, where you want to go, and how you'd get there — so by the end it's clear, and if you like what you hear, we'll make you an offer." |

Notice the shared skeleton: warm open → "here's how this'll work" → "if it's a fit, great; if not, no worries" → "sound good?" Only the emphasis changes. Owen even admits some of these are too long — shorter is usually better.

> 🔑 **Frame is one idea in five costumes: *I'm leading, here's the plan, and either outcome is fine.* Say it in the accent of their energy type.**

## Key takeaways

1. **The master framework is Rapport → Frame → Discovery → Pitch → Close.** This lesson owns the first two.
2. **Go scriptless for your first ~30 calls.** Presence beats memorization; build a loose framework later, never a script.
3. **Rapport in three steps:** friendly intro, chameleon-match, cliché question — kept short.
4. **Read PACE early.** Most B2B owners are Practical or Action. It's a helper, not a rule, and never a stereotype.
5. **Generate energy for flat prospects.** The power plant generates energy; so do you. But don't force "bro energy."
6. **Frame = whoever asks the questions leads.** Set the agenda, adapt the tone to the energy type, hold it calmly.

## Common pitfalls

- ❌ **Overbuilding rapport.** 8–15 minutes of friendliness kills your frame and your nerve. Keep it to a few minutes.
- ❌ **Mirroring low energy.** Matching a flat prospect drags the whole call down. Lift them instead.
- ❌ **Forcing bro energy.** "What's good, bro?" to a formal 50-year-old is an instant mismatch. Only match it if they start it.
- ❌ **Reading a frame set aloud.** The five sets are illustrations, not scripts. A read frame sounds fake and blows the sale.
- ❌ **Absorbing disrespect to be "nice."** Late, distracted, unprepared prospects don't magically close. Reset frame or reschedule.
- ❌ **Confusing frame with domination.** Talking over real questions isn't holding frame; it's steamrolling — and it costs you the retention.

## 🛠️ Capstone Project: Your PACE-adaptive opening

> This is the main hands-on project for the lesson. You'll walk into your next mock call with a real opening you can run on autopilot, so your attention is free to actually *read the person*.

### What you will build

A one-page opening kit for **your** niche (the local business type you chose earlier in the course), containing:

- A **three-line rapport opener** (intro + chameleon note + one cliché question), written in your own natural voice.
- **PACE cues for your niche** — the keywords/vibes that signal each type, and one line on how you'd shift for P vs A (your two most likely types).
- **Three short frame sets** — general, practical, action — in bullet form, as *cues to hit*, not scripts to read.
- A **personal disrespect rule**: your exact words for resetting frame and for rescheduling an unprepared prospect.

### Why this is the perfect practice

| Lesson idea | Where you use it in the opening kit |
|---|---|
| Three-step rapport | The rapport opener |
| PACE reading | The niche cue list |
| Chameleon / generate energy | The P-vs-A shift note |
| Frame = set the agenda | The three frame sets |
| Never accept disrespect | The personal rule |

### Milestones (build them in order, each one works on its own)

1. **Write the rapport opener.** Three lines: a warm intro, a note on matching energy, and one cliché question. Say it out loud until it sounds like you.
2. **List PACE cues for your niche.** For a roofer, a plumber, a med-spa owner — what does a Practical one sound like vs an Action one? One or two keywords each.
3. **Draft three frame sets as bullet cues.** General, Practical, Action. Bullets only — if it reads like a paragraph you'll be tempted to recite it.
4. **Write your disrespect rule.** One reset line ("give me one second…") and one reschedule line, in your own words, that you'd actually be willing to say.
5. **Rehearse three mock openings.** Ideally with a friend playing (a) a flat/low-energy owner, (b) a bro-energy owner, (c) an ego'd older owner. Just rapport + frame, then stop.
6. **Debrief each mock.** Note: did you generate energy or mirror it? Did you hold frame or get led? One fix per rep.
7. **Stretch goals.** Add a Social and Emotional frame set; record yourself and listen back for tonality (upward-curious vs downward-authoritative).

### How you will know you are done

- ✅ You can run rapport + frame in under five minutes without reading anything.
- ✅ You can name the P and A cues for your niche from memory.
- ✅ You have said your reschedule line out loud and mean it.
- ✅ In at least one mock, a low-energy "prospect" visibly warmed up.

> 💡 **Keep yourself honest:** if any frame set is a paragraph you'd *read*, you've failed the assignment. Cut it to cues.

## Practice exercises (optional extra reps)

> **What these are:** small, self-contained tasks, each giving focused practice on one idea. Optional and independent; the Capstone already touches all of them, so feel free to skip straight to it.

### Exercise 1: Tone twins (foundational)
Take one sentence ("It's nice to put a name to the face"). Record it twice — once flat, once warm and curious. Play both back. Hear the difference Owen keeps hammering.

### Exercise 2: Type-spotting (intermediate)
Watch three sales-call recordings on YouTube. Within the first two minutes, guess each prospect's PACE type and note the keyword that tipped you off. Check yourself as the call unfolds.

### Exercise 3: Hold-the-frame drill (advanced)
Owen's own exercise: hop on a call with a friend, pick a topic, take opposite sides, and argue. Whoever cuts in, redirects, and stays in control "holds frame." Do three rounds; swap who tries to dominate.
