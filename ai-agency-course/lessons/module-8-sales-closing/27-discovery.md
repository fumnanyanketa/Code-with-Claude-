## In one sentence

Discovery is the 15-to-35-minute middle of a sales call where you ask a living line of questions — present, expanding, building emotional momentum — to move a prospect from a vague "I want more money" all the way to "I am the person who has to change," and this lesson teaches you the mechanics while drawing a hard line at manipulation.

> 🎯 **Where this lesson is heading.** It builds to a hands-on **Capstone
> Project** where you build a **discovery question bank for your own niche** — one
> opening question, a level-1 → level-2 → level-3 question ladder, and example
> pre-frames — then run **five mock calls** and self-score how deep you got.
> Everything before the Capstone teaches the skills you will use there. If you
> want to see the finish line first, jump to the **"Capstone Project"** section,
> then come back.

## First-principles companion

> 💡 **The durable idea behind this lesson.** Owen's "discovery" is a repackaging
> of a decades-old, well-documented sales method. For the timeless, tool-agnostic
> version:
>
> - **[SPIN Selling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SPIN_selling)** by Neil Rackham
>   (1988, based on 35,000 sales calls). Its Situation → Problem → Implication →
>   Need-payoff question sequence is the research-backed skeleton under Owen's
>   "level one → two → three." Read it and you will see every move in this lesson,
>   minus the pressure.

## A few plain-language basics first

This lesson uses some everyday terms. Here they are in plain words:

- **Discovery:** the questioning stage of a sales call where you learn the
  prospect's real situation, problems, and goals — the "diagnosis" before any
  "prescription."
- **Prospect:** the potential client on the call. In this course, usually a
  local business owner (a roofer, a dentist, a gym owner).
- **PNA type:** Owen's shorthand for **Practical / No-nonsense / Assertive**
  business owners — older, blunt, ego-forward. Most of your prospects.
- **Pain point:** a specific thing that is going wrong for them and hurts —
  "I only see my kids on Sundays," not "business is tough."
- **Level one / two / three:** how deep the conversation is. **One** = surface
  facts. **Two** = emotional awareness of the problem. **Three** = identity
  ("who I have to become"). You will meet each below.
- **Pre-frame:** a sentence you say *before* a question to set up why you're
  asking it, so it lands as care instead of interrogation.
- **The gap:** the distance between where they are now and where they want to be.
  Your job is to make that gap visible; your offer will later bridge it.

You do not need to memorise these. Each is explained again the first time it matters.

## Why this lesson matters

Owen is blunt about it: *"Discovery is literally where the sale is made. It's
where someone goes from an absolute complete stranger to a paying customer."*
Everything else on the call — rapport, frame, pitch, close — is scaffolding
around this one stretch. Get discovery right and, in his words, at the end
*"you'll just drop the price and they will literally just buy."* Get it wrong —
rush it, stay on the surface — and no pitch can save you. This is also the
lesson where the tactics get powerful enough to be dangerous, so we teach the
moves **and** where to stop.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. Explain why discovery, not the pitch, is where the sale is made, and how long it should run.
2. Run discovery on the three aspects: presence, expansion, and emotional momentum.
3. Open with one big question and correctly read the two responses: vague vs. info-bomb.
4. Handle the vague response (be a doctor, collect data, benchmark KPIs, get ownership of the *situation*).
5. Handle the info-bomb response (convert months to days, dig into *behaviour*).
6. Move a prospect from level one to level three using shifting and identity questions and the one-iceberg rule.
7. Name the ethical lines — manufacturing pain, weaponising insecurity, playing therapist — and stay on the right side of them.

## Part 1: What discovery is, and why it is the whole game

After you set the frame and say *"Cool, if we get started — sound good?"*, you
dive into your first question. From there it is a **series and line of
questioning** that moves them toward wanting to buy. Owen's rule of thumb:
discovery runs **15 to 35 minutes** — the majority of the call.

> 🔑 **If your discovery is two minutes long, you will never close someone —
> there isn't enough emotional momentum or buy-in to make a decision.**

The mental model is two diamonds joined by a bridge. One diamond is **where they
are now**; the other is **where they want to be**. Discovery's job is to make
both crystal-clear so the **gap** between them is undeniable. Your offer (next
lesson) becomes the bridge across that gap.

```text
   WHERE THEY ARE          THE GAP          WHERE THEY WANT TO BE
   ◇ 60k/mo, no time  →  [ you widen ]  →   ◇ 120k/mo, sees kids
   ─────────────────────  bridge  ─────────────────────
                     your offer goes here (Lesson 28)
```

Two ground rules Owen repeats:

- **No scripts.** *"A tactic shown is a tactic blown."* A PNA business owner can
  smell a script, and it kills trust. You use a *framework*, not lines.
- **It should feel like a conversation, not a Q&A.** Two people talking, not an
  interrogation with a clipboard.

> ⚠️ **Reality check.** "Where the sale is made" is a seller's framing. Discovery
> is genuinely useful — it's how you find out whether you can actually help. Keep
> *that* as the real goal. If, mid-discovery, it becomes clear you can't help this
> person, the honest move is to say so, not to keep digging for a sale.

## Part 2: Aspect #1 — Presence (build the next question on their answer)

If Owen could give you one word for discovery, it's **presence**.

> 🔑 **"When you are there on the call and you are listening to them… you will
> never forget what to say. You'll never stumble. You'll never not know what the
> next question you must be asking is."** — Owen

Here is the insight that dissolves the beginner's #1 fear ("I won't know what to
ask"). You don't know what to ask *because you're in your head hunting for the
next question instead of listening.* When you actually listen, the prospect hands
you **fuel** — a phrase, a number, a sigh — and the next question is obvious: it's
built on what they just said.

| Weak rep (scripted) | Strong rep (present) |
|---|---|
| Asks Q1, hears answer, jumps to Q2 on the list | Asks Q1, *hears* the answer, asks a Q2 built **on** that answer |
| Conversation stays surface-level | Conversation goes deeper each turn |
| Misses the fuel | Catches the fuel and pushes on it |

> 💡 **Trust is built with presence, not just testimonials.** Social proof helps
> "to an extent," Owen says, but *"if you have unwavering presence, that is
> literally all the trust that you'll need."*

**The opening question.** Start every discovery with the *same* solid, heavy
opener — a big question that skips the surface. Examples to adapt (illustrative,
not a script):

- *"What's the biggest problem in your sales process right now?"*
- *"What's the biggest bottleneck in your lead-flow right now?"*

Why big and why first? Because your setter already logged the surface facts
(rough revenue, lead counts), so re-asking them wastes the opener. A big question
positions you as an expert and *maximises the chance you jump straight toward
level two.* It produces one of **two responses** — and the rest of the lesson is
how you handle each.

## Part 3: The two responses — vague vs. info-bomb

```text
        BIG OPENING QUESTION
                 │
      ┌──────────┴───────────┐
   VAGUE                  INFO-BOMB
 "I just want         "I'm underpaid, working
  more money"          80hr weeks, never see
                       my kids, only 60k/mo…"
      │                        │
 They own NOTHING yet.   They already own the
 Goal: get ownership     SITUATION. Goal: get
 of the SITUATION.       ownership of BEHAVIOUR.
```

Read this fork correctly, because the two paths are almost opposite. Misread it —
run the vague playbook on an info-bomb prospect — and, in Owen's experience, they
get annoyed: *"Why do you need to know all these facts about my life? I know I
need to change — let's dive into how you can help me."*

## Part 4: Handling the VAGUE response — be a doctor

They gave you nothing real ("I want more leads"). They have **no ownership** of
their situation yet — no responsibility, no control, no emotional stake. Your job:
become a **doctor** and collect data.

**Step 1 — collect surface data (level one).** Ask a lot of low-stakes questions
and find the issue: what they do, how many leads they get, how many jobs they
close, what they're paid, whether they're underpaid, whether partners split the
profit, how much time they get with family.

**Step 2 — go for exact numbers, and benchmark them.** Most local owners don't
know their own KPIs — close rate, average job value, customer lifetime value
(LTV, the total a customer is worth over time). *"They don't know these numbers,
right?"* You should. Build a **benchmark of KPIs for your niche** ahead of time —
what close rate, booking rate, job value, and margin *should* look like (you can
draft these with ChatGPT/Claude and refine them). Now you can hear their number
and know instantly if it's healthy. That's what makes you the expert.

> ✅ **What to do about it:** Before your next call, write a one-page KPI
> benchmark for your niche. When they can't answer "what's your close rate?", you
> calmly supplying the healthy range *is* your authority.

**Step 3 — get ownership of the situation.** The whole point of the vague path is
to make them *unsell themselves* on what they're currently doing — to realise the
status quo isn't working — so they take ownership of a situation they'd been
rationalising away. A gentle challenge that jumps them toward level two:

> *"Do you feel like part of the reason your lead generation isn't where you want
> it to be is because you're not actually aware of what your conversion looks
> like?"*

> ❌ **Don't challenge too early.** Hit them with a hard mirror in the first two
> minutes and it becomes an argument. Collect data first; earn the right to
> challenge.

## Part 5: Aspect #2 — Expansion (hold up the mirror)

Once they own the situation, **expand**. Presence lets you *catch* pain points;
expansion lets you *push on them*. You ask increasingly specific questions and
build on each answer, creating emotional weight:

- *"How many leads exactly did you get this month? What about last month? The month before?"* (If they give a range, push for the exact number.)
- *"What have you tried in the past? How did it go? Why didn't it work? How long did you give it?"*
- *"If you continue at this pace of being underpaid, how long are you going to let it slip by — another year?"*

This is **holding up the mirror**: you don't *tell* them their situation is
painful — that triggers defensiveness — you ask questions whose answers make
*them* see it. **They must reach the realisation themselves.**

**Two things make expansion work without blowing up:**

**1. Openness / a permission pre-frame.** Before you push hard, the prospect has
to understand you're about to show them some intense truths *because you believe
in a better future for them.* Without that, the mirror becomes an argument —
especially with PNA types. Owen's example pre-frame:

> *"Listen, I'm going to give you some truths here that are pretty intense — but
> I'm doing it because I want you to get this result."*

**2. Pre-framing between questions.** Amateurs go *"Question. Okay. Question.
Okay."* — a robotic Q&A. Pre-framing is a sentence that sets up the next question
so it lands as care, not interrogation. Owen's line: whether you pre-frame well
*"is kind of where it shows whether or not you give a damn."*

> 💡 **Tone and intention are the whole thing.** Anything can sound "salesy" or
> "invasive" — it's *how it comes across.* If you genuinely want this person
> better off, the undertone takes care of itself.

### Truth-telling and calling out BS

With PNA owners, *the truth alone is usually enough to create the pain.* But you
only get the truth if you **call out BS** — they lie about their numbers
constantly. Owen's classic:

| Prospect says | Weak rep | Strong rep |
|---|---|---|
| "I work 120 hours a week." | "Wow, you work really hard." | "Wait — 17 hours a day? So you sleep 7 hours, don't eat, don't go to the bathroom, and just work? How much do you *actually* work a day?" |

The strong rep isn't being cruel — they're refusing to accept an inflated number,
which surfaces the *real* situation and, with it, real emotional weight. If you
let them lie comfortably to you, *"they will literally just lie to get off the
call."*

> ⚠️ **The line, part 1.** "Hold up the mirror so they see they're the problem"
> and "call out their BS" are legitimate *only* when you're pushing back on
> genuine exaggeration in service of a problem you can truly fix. The moment you
> find yourself *manufacturing* pain that isn't there, or leaning on someone's
> insecurities to get a card number, you've crossed from selling into
> manipulation. Only sell to people you can actually help.

## Part 6: Aspect #3 — Emotional momentum (energy with direction)

The third aspect: **emotional momentum** — Owen calls it *"an energy transfer with
direction,"* the direction being toward the sale. You **layer** truths to build
it. The technique is to go *back in time* and stack exact numbers:

> *"How much money did you make this month — exactly?"* → *"What about last month?
> The month before?"*

Each exact number (not a range) that confirms things aren't okay adds momentum and
nudges them further into ownership. Anything that breaks the line — a tangent, a
phone call, a wander — *hurts* the momentum. Your job is to keep the energy moving
along the bridge.

> 🔑 **"It's very hard to close when there's no friction… Friction is literally
> where the sale is made."** — Owen. His repeated rule: it is far better to push
> hard and *pull back* than to be timid and never push. Pulling back is easy;
> closing with zero friction is nearly impossible.

The pull-back valve, when it gets too heated:

> *"Do you even know why I'm having this conversation with you today? It's because
> I actually care and want to see you get better results."*

## Part 7: Handling the INFO-BOMB response — ownership of behaviour

Now the other fork. They answered the big opener with a flood of fuel — underpaid,
overworked, no family time, only 60k/month. **They already own the situation.** So
you do *not* run the doctor/data playbook — that annoys them. Instead:

**1. Find the moment of realisation.** *"When did you realise this had to change?"*

**2. Convert the timeframe to days.** If they say "about nine months," you say
**270 days**. Keep a calculator ready for calls.

> 🔑 **"270 days sounds much more visceral than nine months."** — Owen

**3. Dig into behaviour since then.** *"So it's been 270 days — what have you
actually done since then to fix it?"* The answer is usually *nothing much*, and
that's the point: you're now holding up a different mirror.

| Vague response | Info-bomb response |
|---|---|
| Mirror shows: **the situation** is bad | Mirror shows: **you** are the problem |
| Goal: own the **situation** | Goal: own the **behaviour** |
| They haven't admitted things are bad | They've admitted it but haven't *acted* |

Owen's framing: real change needs ownership of *two* things — the **situation** and
the **behaviour**. The vague prospect lacks the first; the info-bomb prospect has
the first but lacks the second. Questions that hold them accountable:

- *"You said you couldn't be present with your partner anymore, and it's been 270 days. Why have you let these days pass by?"*
- *"Do you think people have made this change in less than 270 days? What are those people doing that you aren't?"*
- *"What's actually missing for you to make this transition?"*

> ⚠️ **The line, part 2.** Converting months to days *purely to make a number feel
> heavier* is manipulation of perception. It's fine to help someone feel the real
> cost of inaction; it's not fine to inflate that cost dishonestly. And "you are
> the problem" is a heavy thing to tell a stranger — you are a marketer, **not a
> licensed coach or therapist.** If someone is genuinely in distress, the move is
> compassion and stepping back, not deeper digging.

## Part 8: Level three — identity and the one-iceberg rule

As the gap widens you shift from level two (emotional awareness) toward **level
three: identity** — where the prospect takes **100% responsibility** and the
conversation becomes about *who they have to be.* You do this with **shifting
questions** and **identity questions** ("I am…"):

- *"Do you feel like you're a million-a-year business owner? Do you think you even deserve that?"*
- *"What's actually going to change once you hit a million a year?"*
- *"We're in the business of taking people from good to great. So what does *great* look like for you?"*

Remember most owners aren't going 0 → 5k; they're going 50k → 100k. Level three is
where you gently **cast an identity** they then try to live up to.

**The iceberg principle — dig one pain to the bottom.** When they hand you three
pain points (kids, money, retiring their parents), a bad rep skips between all
three, staying shallow on each. Do the opposite:

> 🔑 **"Each pain point is a mini iceberg. Dig on one till the bottom — till
> there's nothing left — before you ever switch."** — Owen

Switching pain points feels like progress but keeps you on the surface. Depth on
*one* iceberg is what creates the emotionally-buying state.

```text
BAD: •—kids  •—money  •—parents   (three shallow scratches)
GOOD:  •—kids
        └─ can't coach the team
           └─ missed the championship
              └─ "he stopped asking me to come"   ← bottom of the iceberg
```

> ⚠️ **The line, part 3.** Challenging someone's identity — "do you even deserve
> that?" — is the most powerful and most dangerous move here. Used well, it helps
> a capable person stop playing small. Used badly, it's just weaponising
> insecurity to override someone's judgement. Ask yourself honestly: am I helping
> them decide, or overpowering their ability to decide? If it's the second, stop.
> Pressure-selling also wrecks the thing your whole agency runs on — retention and
> local reputation. A client bullied into buying churns and tells the town.

## Key takeaways

1. **Discovery is the meat of the call (15–35 min).** Two minutes of it and you cannot close.
2. **Presence beats scripts.** Build every question on their last answer; the fuel they give you *is* the next question.
3. **One big opening question forks two ways.** Vague → get ownership of the *situation* (be a doctor). Info-bomb → get ownership of *behaviour* (dig their actions).
4. **Expansion = hold up the mirror.** Specific questions + pre-frames make *them* see the pain; never announce it for them.
5. **Emotional momentum is energy with direction.** Layer exact numbers back in time; kill tangents.
6. **Dig one iceberg to the bottom.** Depth on one pain beats a shallow tour of three.
7. **Only sell to people you can genuinely help, and stay in your lane** — you're a marketer, not a therapist.

## Common pitfalls

- ❌ **Rushing discovery to get to the pitch.** No momentum, no sale. Slow down; 15–35 minutes.
- ❌ **Reading questions off a list.** A tactic shown is a tactic blown; you'll miss all the fuel. Listen instead.
- ❌ **Running the doctor/data playbook on an info-bomb prospect.** They'll ask why you need all these facts. Skip to behaviour.
- ❌ **Challenging too early, before any data or openness.** It becomes an argument, especially with PNA types.
- ❌ **Jumping between pain points.** Shallow everywhere. Pick one iceberg and go to the bottom.
- ❌ **Manufacturing pain, inflating numbers dishonestly, or weaponising insecurities.** That's manipulation, not selling — and it torches retention and reputation.
- ❌ **Playing amateur therapist.** If someone's in real distress, step back with care. You are not licensed for that.

## 🛠️ Capstone Project: Your niche discovery question bank + 5 mock calls

> This is the main hands-on project for the lesson. You'll turn Owen's scriptless
> approach into a *framework* of your own and then rep it live, so on real calls
> you're present instead of panicking.

### What you will build

A one-page **discovery framework** for the exact niche your agency serves (e.g.
roofers, med-spas, gyms), plus a **scorecard** you fill in after five mock calls.
Its pieces, each mapped to a lesson idea:

- **One big opening question** — Part 2.
- **A level-1 → level-2 → level-3 question ladder** — Parts 4, 5, 8.
- **A KPI benchmark table for the niche** — Part 4.
- **3–4 example pre-frames** — Part 5.
- **A vague-path branch and an info-bomb branch** — Part 3.
- **A 5-call self-scoring sheet** — the whole lesson.

### Why this is the perfect practice

| Lesson idea | Where you use it in the question bank |
|---|---|
| Presence | You write *ladders*, not scripts — prompts that build on answers |
| Big opening question | The single opener at the top of the bank |
| Vague vs. info-bomb | Two branches you can jump between mid-call |
| Doctor / KPI benchmark | The benchmark table you draft with an AI and refine |
| Hold up the mirror + pre-frame | The pre-frame lines and mirror questions |
| Months → days, layering | A behaviour-branch prompt sheet + a calculator to hand |
| Iceberg / identity | Level-3 identity questions and a "dig deeper" reminder |
| Ethics | A one-line "can I actually help this person?" check at the top |

### Milestones (build them in order, each one works on its own)

1. **Pick your niche and write the ethics check.** One line at the top of the page: *"Can I genuinely get this business a result? If not, I refer out."*
2. **Write your one big opening question.** Adapt Owen's — biggest bottleneck / biggest problem — to your niche's language.
3. **Draft the KPI benchmark table.** Use ChatGPT or Claude to get healthy ranges (close rate, booking rate, average job value, margin, LTV) for your niche, then sanity-check them. Five rows is enough.
4. **Build the level-1 ladder (vague branch).** 6–8 surface data questions that end in exact numbers.
5. **Build the level-2 ladder.** Mirror questions + 3 pre-frame lines + the "openness" permission line.
6. **Build the level-3 ladder + info-bomb branch.** Identity questions, and the behaviour sequence (realisation → days → what have you done → why not).
7. **Run 5 mock calls and score them.** Friend, peer, or an AI role-playing a business owner. After each, log: which level did I reach (1/2/3)? Did I dig one iceberg to the bottom? Did I stay ethical?
8. **Stretch goals.** Record one mock call and re-listen for the fuel you missed. Then run one call *without looking at the page* — the real test of a framework vs. a script.

### How you will know you are done

- ✅ Your framework fits on one page and contains no full-sentence "script" — only prompts and branches.
- ✅ You can name, for any answer a prospect gives, which of the two branches you're on.
- ✅ Across five mock calls, your self-scored depth reached **level three at least twice**.
- ✅ At least once you caught and called out an inflated number without it becoming a fight.
- ✅ You can state in one sentence where *you* would stop pushing and why.

> 💡 **Keep yourself honest:** score depth *after* each call, before you forget.
> A framework you can't run without reading is still a script — the goal is to
> not need the page.

## Practice exercises (optional extra reps)

> **What these are:** small, self-contained tasks, each giving focused practice
> on one idea. Optional and independent; the Capstone already touches all of
> them, so feel free to skip straight to it.

### Exercise 1: The presence drill (foundational)
Have a friend give you a one-line business complaint. Ask a question built *only*
on their words. Repeat for five turns without ever using a pre-planned question.
Notice how the next question is always sitting in their last answer.

### Exercise 2: Fork-the-response (intermediate)
Write two different first answers to your big opener — one vague, one info-bomb.
For each, write the very next thing you'd say. If your two "next things" are the
same, you haven't internalised the fork yet.

### Exercise 3: Iceberg dig (advanced)
Take one pain point ("I never see my kids") and write a five-question chain that
digs *straight down* on it — no switching topics. Each question must reference the
previous answer. Stop only when there's nothing left to reach.
