A few plain-language basics first

This lesson uses some everyday sales terms. Here they are in plain words:

  • Setting: the act of booking someone onto a later sales call. The person who does it is a setter (also called an SDR, sales development rep).
  • Closer: the person on the later call who actually asks for the money. On a small agency, you are usually both — you set and close.
  • Show rate: the percentage of booked appointments where the prospect actually turns up. A booking that no-shows is worth nothing.
  • Frame: the unspoken "who's in charge here" of a conversation. Holding frame means you're the one steering, not being steered.
  • B2B / B2C: business-to-business (you're selling to a business owner) vs. business-to-consumer (you're selling to an individual). Your setting targets are almost always B2B.

You do not need to memorise these. Each is re-explained the first time it bites.

Why this lesson matters

Owen is blunt about where show rate comes from: "How you show up to the call based on the four types of communication calibrates how or even if the prospect shows up to the sales call in the first place. It's not the reminders, it's not the automations." — Owen. In other words, a no-show is usually a setting failure, not a calendar failure. If you can learn to sound like a confident human who's screening people (not begging for a slot), your appointments start showing up — and a shown appointment is the raw fuel every later lesson runs on.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. Define setting, distinguish setter from closer, and explain why show rate is mostly the setter's skill.
  2. Adopt the setter identity: intentionality, assuming the sale, calm authority, and detachment.
  3. Use tonality correctly — upward for questions, downward for statements, warm-hug energy, never robotic.
  4. Apply frames, labels, and mirrors to move a conversation toward the booking.
  5. Ask open and double-commitment questions, and know when double-commitment questions fail on cold calls.
  6. Spot red flags, break patterns, and use humor to earn the appointment.

Part 1: What "setting" is, and why the show rate is on you

Setting is booking appointments — but the good definition adds three words: appointments that show up. Owen's split is simple: a setter books the call; a closer takes it and asks for payment. Early on you are both, but the mindset is different in each seat, so it helps to name them.

Here's the counter-intuitive part. Most beginners think a no-show is bad luck or a weak reminder email. Owen disagrees hard: a warm-hug, confident setter can double their show rate over a robotic one booking the exact same leads. The booking isn't the finish line — a booking a prospect forgets by lunchtime is worthless. The finish line is a prospect who shows.

🔑 You are the first experience someone has with your brand. Your job is not to fill a calendar slot — it's to make the prospect feel awarded a spot on the next call.

A great setter, in Owen's words, doesn't beg for calendar slots — "You award them." — Owen. That framing shift (screening people in vs. begging them in) is the whole game, and everything below serves it.

Part 2: The setter identity — who you are off the call

"Who you are off the call is who you are on the call." — Owen

This is Owen's load-bearing claim, and he repeats it on purpose. Your internal beliefs travel down the phone line through tone and posture — yes, even over the phone. So the identity work below isn't fluff; it's the thing prospects actually feel. Five pieces:

1. Intentionality. Set a clear intention before you dial. Owen's exact framing, worth writing on your wall:

✅ "I am simply going to determine whether the prospect needs my agency's help and if they have the necessary time, budget, and resources to benefit from my services." — Owen

If your real intention is just "get a name on the calendar," they feel it and they don't show. If it's "find out whether I can genuinely help this person," your show rate jumps. Same words on the call; different result — because intention leaks through everything.

2. Assuming the sale. Operate on the belief that they'll move forward unless disqualified. It's the difference between "if we work together…" and "when we get started…" — Owen. The "if" language quietly signals you don't believe it yourself, and prospects catch it. Talk in "when," not "if."

3. Authority. Not loud, not rude — "calm, measured, commanding." — Owen. Authority is knowing when to interrupt a pointless tangent, when to redirect, and being the one asking the questions. Lose the frame and you lose: they won't agree, and if they do, they won't show.

4. Detachment and state management. Detach from yes/no. A brutal hang-up is just words. The skill, Owen says, is doing "10 more calls after that" happy and present. His trick: become an observer — get curious about the situation ("huh, I wonder why that person reacted that way") instead of taking it personally. Between rough calls, take three deep breaths and say your three anchor words to reset your state.

5. The if/how call frame (expectation setting). On warm inbound calls only, you can say: "This is just an 'if' call to see if we're a good fit — and if we are, I'll transfer you to the 'how' call where we show you how it works." It sets a clean expectation. But keep setting calls casual overall; don't over-frame.

💡 When NOT to challenge. On closing calls (and B2C) you challenge the prospect hard. On B2B setting, don't. You don't have enough authority yet — they clock that you're younger, assume less experience, and hang up. Save challenging for later.

💡 Earning the right to drop the script. Owen is anti-script: you start with a script, graduate to a loose framework, then to nothing. But you only earn scriptless setting through reps. A buried-in-a-script setter misses the emotional cues that a present one dives into. Start scripted, aim to drop it.

⚠️ Reality check. "Assuming the sale" and setting the frame are genuine persuasion tools — and they sit right next to manipulation. The honest line is Owen's own rule: only book people you can actually help. Assumptive certainty is fine when you truly can deliver; it's manipulation when you're just steering a stranger onto a calendar to hit a number. Keep it honest.

Part 3: The communication toolbox

Identity gets them to feel you. These tools get them to book. Every example line below is illustrative — a pattern to adapt to your niche and your voice, never a script to read word-for-word.

Tonality — the single biggest lever

Owen calls tonality "the most important thing out of all this entire video." The rule of thumb:

  • Upward inflection for questions and curiosity: "So… what are your goals for this year?"
  • Downward inflection for statements and certainty: "Here's what we do. We help roofers bring in more work using paid ads."

Warm-hug energy, not a business robot. Owen's robot impression — flat, no inflection, "Hey, is this John? We help roofers get more roof replacements…" — is exactly what gets you hung up on. Be a normal person the owner would want to be friends with. Crack a joke. And remember: on a setting call you're not pitching the service, you're pitching the second call. Pitch the service and they say "not interested" and hang up.

Frames, labels, and mirrors

Tool What it does Illustrative example
Frame Re-say what they said, aimed toward the sale They: "I don't need more work." You: "Sounds like you've achieved what there is to achieve there — maybe it's time for something bigger."
Label Name their identity, usually with "sounds like…" They: "I've been up since 6 a.m., don't even see the family." You: "Sounds like you're a real action-taker."
Mirror Repeat their last 1–3 words, upward, to get more They: "Honestly I'm drained." You: "Drained…?" → they explain further.

❌ Don't mirror the whole sentence ("You're going bankrupt?") — it sounds like an attack. Mirror only the last word or two, with curiosity, never judgment.

Open questions (non-yes/no)

Yes/no questions stall the call. "Is business going well?" gets a dead "yes." "How's business going?" gets a real answer you can work with. Ask what, how, and why: "What has you looking into this?" / "In an ideal world, what's your goal with lead generation?"

Double-commitment questions

A question with two paths, where both answers lead toward the sale. Great as openers: "Are you currently in real estate, or just looking to get into it?" / "Have you dipped your toes into lead generation, or looking to get started?" Whichever they pick, you're moving forward.

❌ They don't work on cold. On a cold call the prospect has zero affiliation with you — "Are you looking to get started?" makes no sense because they've never heard of you. Double-commitment openers shine on paid ads / inbound ("you filled out our ad, right?"), sometimes on cold SMS if done carefully — almost never on a cold call.

Red-flag recognition

Watch for: "I won't pay upfront no matter what," people who brag about money or fixate on price, people who seem plainly broke, and anyone for whom marketing obviously won't work. A red flag isn't an automatic hang-up — keep going, but skeptically. If it's a hard red flag, don't waste the booking.

Pattern breaking and humor

Owners get 5–10 identical robot calls a day, so the first ~5 seconds decide whether you're hung up on. Break the pattern — sound different, be human, be funny. Humor builds rapport fast: "Why are you different from the ten others who called me today?" → "Honestly, I ask myself that same question every day and I still can't figure it out." Get them laughing and they'll take a meeting because they like you.

💡 On "reverse qualification" and brush-offs. Owen teaches "reverse qualification" — acting mildly unsure they even qualify ("I don't know if we can even help you…") so they sell you. It can work, but it's a manipulation of the frame, and lines like "if not, I probably couldn't help you anyway" are pressure brush-offs. Use the lightest version, with warm tone, and only on people you'd genuinely be glad to work with. Pressure-selling wins a booking and loses a reputation.

Key takeaways

  1. A no-show is a setting failure. Show rate lives mostly in how you show up, not in reminders or automations.
  2. Identity first, tactics second. Intention, assuming the sale, calm authority, and detachment are what prospects actually feel.
  3. Tonality is the biggest single lever. Up for questions, down for statements, warm-hug energy, never robotic.
  4. Pitch the second call, not the service. The setting call's only job is to award the next step.
  5. Match the tool to the temperature. Double-commitment openers and "why now" are for warm/inbound — not cold calls.
  6. Only book people you can help. The persuasion tools slide into manipulation the moment you forget this.

Common pitfalls

  • ❌ Reading a script robotically. Flat tone gets you hung up on. Start scripted if you must, but sound like a human and aim to drop it.
  • ❌ Begging for the slot. "Any chance you'd have five minutes sometime?" signals low status. Award the appointment; don't beg for it.
  • ❌ Pitching the service on the setting call. Triggers "not interested." Pitch only the next call.
  • ❌ Double-commitment openers on a cold call. They collapse without prior affiliation. Keep them for inbound.
  • ❌ Challenging a B2B owner while setting. You lack the authority; you'll get pushback or a hang-up. Save it for closing.
  • ❌ Confusing assumptive certainty with pressure. Steering a stranger you can't help onto a calendar isn't confidence — it's manipulation.

🛠️ Capstone Project: Your setter-identity one-pager and toolbox

This is the main hands-on project for the lesson. You'll build the actual artifact a real setter keeps taped to the wall, then pressure-test it in 5 mock calls. Small on purpose — you'll feel the identity shift by call five.

What you will build

A single printable page plus a short "toolbox," then a practice log. The pieces:

  • An intention statement (adapt Owen's, in your own words) — maps to intentionality.
  • 3 anchor words that reset your state — maps to detachment / state management.
  • A toolbox of 3 openers, 3 mirrors, and 3 double-commitment questions for your niche — maps to the communication tools.
  • A 5-call mock log — maps to reps, the only way any of this internalises.

Why this is the perfect practice

Lesson idea Where you use it in the Capstone
Intentionality Your written intention statement
Detachment / state Your 3 anchor words
Tonality Read your openers aloud, up vs. down
Frames / labels / mirrors Your 3 mirror lines
Double-commitment (warm only) Your 3 questions, tagged cold vs. warm
Assuming the sale "When," not "if," in every line you write

Milestones (build them in order, each one works on its own)

  1. Write your intention statement. One or two sentences, your own words, based on "I'm simply determining if they need help and have the time, budget, and resources." Print it.
  2. Pick your 3 anchor words. Words that snap you into a calm, confident state. Add "three deep breaths" as your reset ritual.
  3. Draft 3 openers for your niche. Pattern-breaking, warm, pitching the second call. Mark each as cold-safe or warm-only.
  4. Draft 3 mirror lines and 3 label lines. Last-word mirrors; "sounds like…" labels aimed at your ideal-client identity.
  5. Draft 3 double-commitment questions. Tag which channel each works on — remember: not cold calls.
  6. Run 5 mock setting calls. With a friend, or record yourself both sides. After each, log: tonality (up/down right?), did you assume the sale, did you pitch the call not the service, one thing to fix.
  7. Stretch goals. Add a humor line and a red-flag list for your niche. Re-run call five and compare your show-ready confidence to call one.

How you will know you are done

  • ✅ Your one-pager fits on a single printed page and you'd actually pin it up.
  • ✅ Every written line uses "when," not "if."
  • ✅ Each double-commitment question is tagged cold/warm correctly.
  • ✅ You completed 5 logged mock calls with a fix noted for each.

💡 Keep yourself honest: for every prospect in your mocks, ask "could I actually help this person?" If the honest answer is no, practise not booking them. The identity you're building only works if it's trustworthy.

Practice exercises (optional extra reps)

What these are: small, self-contained tasks, each drilling one idea. Optional and independent; the Capstone already touches all of them.

Exercise 1: Up or down? (foundational)

Take ten of your own lines and mark each as a question (upward) or a statement (downward). Read them aloud both ways and hear the difference.

Exercise 2: Mirror drill (intermediate)

Have someone feed you ten emotional statements ("I'm swamped," "money's tight"). Reply with a one-to-three-word mirror each time. Notice how they keep talking.

Exercise 3: Cold vs. warm sort (advanced)

Write six double-commitment questions and six "why now" questions. Sort each into the channel it works on (cold call / cold SMS / paid ads / organic). Justify each.
