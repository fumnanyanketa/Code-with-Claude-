In one sentence

Almost every growth problem you will ever have is solved by doing more of what already works, occasionally by doing it better, and almost never by doing something new — and the handful of people who fail do so by breaking five avoidable rules, not because the model is broken.

🎯 Where this lesson is heading. It builds to a hands-on Capstone Project where you build a one-page Growth-Diagnosis Card and sign a written pre-commitment against the five failure patterns — two artifacts you will physically reach for every time you feel stuck or panicked in the months ahead. Everything before the Capstone teaches the thinking those artifacts encode. If you want to see the finish line first, jump to the "Capstone Project" section, then come back.

First-principles companion

💡 The durable idea behind this lesson. Owen is upfront that he did not invent this — and neither the tools nor the year matter to the underlying logic. For the timeless, source version:

  • $100M Leads by Alex Hormozi (book). Hormozi frames all business growth as three levers — get more, get them to buy more (better), or open a new channel — and argues most operators reach for "new" when they have not come close to exhausting "more." That is the first-principles account of everything in this lesson. Owen says it plainly: "this is from Alex... I can't take credit for this, from his $100 million leads book." — Owen

A few plain-language basics first

This lesson uses some everyday terms. Here they are in plain words:

  • Lever of growth: any single action you can push on to get a bigger result — spend, conversion rate, number of outreach messages. Pulling a lever = choosing where to put your effort.
  • Volume: how much of a working action you do. More ad spend, more calls, more messages. "Volume is 90% of the issue" is the spine of this lesson.
  • Unit economics: the profit math on a single unit of activity — e.g. you spend $200 in ads to book one appointment that earns you $400. If a unit is profitable, "do more" prints money; if it loses money, "do more" multiplies the loss.
  • Ad set / campaign: the container inside an ad platform (like Meta) that holds an ad, its budget, and its audience. "Killing a campaign" means switching it off.
  • Niche vs. micro-niche: a niche is an industry (home improvement). A micro-niche is a small slice of it (garage doors, home security). Smaller = easier to dominate.
  • First-party data: what you learn by actually running the thing, not what a video told you. Owen's word for hard-won experience.

You do not need to memorise these. Each is explained again the first time it matters.

Why this lesson matters

In Lessons 1 and 2 you learned what this business is and how to filter signal from noise. This lesson gives you the single decision rule you will use for the next several years to answer the question that stops most beginners cold: "It's kind of working — now what do I change?" Owen's answer is almost always "nothing — do more of it." He says this framework "has completely changed my entire life... my entire way of looking at the world is honestly different." — Owen. The reason it matters so much is that the wrong instinct here (chase something new the moment you see success) is exactly the instinct that kills momentum. Getting this one rule right is worth more than any tactic later in the course.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. State the three growth levers — More, Better, New — and explain why, from $0 to $1M, you almost always pull More or Better.
  2. Apply the one-question decision rule ("will doing more volume solve it?") to any growth or constraint problem.
  3. Name the precondition that makes "just do more" safe, and check for it before scaling.
  4. Recognise and pre-empt the five failure patterns that account for nearly all failures in this model.
  5. Produce a reusable Growth-Diagnosis Card and a signed pre-commitment against those five failures.

Part 1: The three levers of growth

Owen borrows the frame directly from Alex Hormozi's $100M Leads. Any time you want growth — more customers, better client results, more money — you can only ever pull one of three levers:

Lever What it means Example in your agency
More Do more of what already works Your ads are profitable → raise the ad spend
Better Same activity, improved output Fix the one weak metric so each dollar does 2–3×
New A whole new offer, niche, or channel Add cold email, add Google reviews, switch niches

The trap is that your brain is drawn to New. Owen puts it bluntly: "Something weird about your brain just fails to see the simple solution of just doing either more or doing something better." — Owen. People booking good calls on profitable ads will suddenly think "let me do cold email too, let me do cold calling too" — when the obvious move was to keep doing the thing that was working, just more of it.

🔑 Three levers exist, but only two are for you right now: More and Better. New is a trap disguised as ambition.

Why New is the rarest lever

For companies from $0 to $1M a year — where you are — Owen is categorical: "you will always either do more or better. Very rarely will you do something completely new." — Owen. In $100M Leads terms, "New" is what you reach for only after you have genuinely exhausted More and Better, "which 99% of the time isn't done." Adding a new channel does not just fail to help — it adds complexity and splits your focus, which is exactly what a beginner cannot afford.

Part 2: Volume is 90% of the issue

If you remember one line from this lesson, make it this one: "volume is 90% of the issue." — Owen. Most of the time there is no clever change to find. There is just not enough of the working thing happening yet.

Owen's favourite example is the panic that follows a first win. You sign your first client or two, and instead of celebrating and repeating, you freeze: "Oh my god, I want to make sure that these guys get good results, so I'm going to stop doing what I did to get these customers and just focus on client results." Owen's response: "Don't do that. Do more of what you did to get those customers." — Owen. The success is the signal. The move after signal is always the same: double down. "Once you find signal, double down on it. Do more of what you did to find success. And that's all you have to do as an entrepreneur." — Owen.

💡 The order within Better and More. When the metric is genuinely below the benchmark you set, you do pull Better first — make the one or two specific fixes that lift a bad number — and only then scale spend. As Owen puts it: get "more output from the same input before you increase the input by doing more." Better is not "change everything"; it is a targeted fix to a named weak metric.

Part 3: The one-question decision rule

Here is the whole framework compressed into a single question you ask before pulling any lever:

PROBLEM / CONSTRAINT
        │
        ▼
  "Will doing more VOLUME solve it?"
        │
   ┌────┴─────┐
  YES         NO
   │           │
   ▼           ▼
 DO MORE   "Can I do it BETTER?"
(scale it)      │
           ┌────┴─────┐
          YES         NO (rare)
           │           │
           ▼           ▼
     fix the      only now
     weak metric,  consider NEW
     then MORE

Owen: "always ask yourself, will just doing more volume or doing more solve it? The answer is almost 100% of the time, yes. If it's not, then do whatever you're doing better. And almost never go to new." — Owen. Notice the questions he wants running in your head at all times are not "what else can I do?" but "why can't I do more? ... why can't I make it better?"

⚠️ Reality check — the precondition nobody says out loud. "Just spend more" only prints money when the unit economics are already profitable. Owen's own setup for the whole framework assumes it: "If you're booking a good amount of calls and you're profitable on your ads..." — Owen. If a unit loses money, doing more simply multiplies the loss faster. So the decision rule has a gate in front of it: first confirm the thing you'd scale is profitable (or clearly on track to be). If it isn't, you are in "Better" territory, not "More." Never scale a leak.

✅ What to do about it: Before you scale anything, write down the unit — "$X in → $Y out per appointment/client." If Y > X, pull More. If Y < X, pull Better until Y > X. Only then scale.

Part 4: The five ways people actually fail

Owen is emphatic that "it's almost never the case that the model doesn't work... It's always because of some avoidable mistake." — Owen. Here are the five he names. Each is a self-inflicted wound, and each has a one-line antidote.

Failure 1 — Managing ads emotionally (not letting them breathe)

"A lot of people will sit in Ads Manager and will constantly refresh it 10 times a day and they'll kill campaigns before they've even had time to spend out of emotion." — Owen. The fix is patience: "You need to let the ads breathe. You need to give them time. You need to let them run." — Owen. Ad platforms need enough spend and time to gather statistically meaningful data; killing a campaign after a few hours is deciding on noise.

Failure 2 — Ignoring data / acting on feelings

"Instead of following the metrics and making decisions off of data... they do it based off of feelings, instincts, emotions... you can't act on feelings. You have to act on metrics. Otherwise, you're just guessing." — Owen. This is the twin of Failure 1: both come from letting emotion, rather than the numbers, drive the decision. Set your target metrics before launch so you have an objective bar to judge against.

Failure 3 — Staying in a niche that is too broad

Some people plant themselves in a big, crowded industry (Owen's early example: roofing) and struggle — "not because the niche is hard necessarily, but because it's so competitive." — Owen. The fix is to niche down to a micro-niche: "work with a small subset of people inside a general larger industry... whether that's landscaping or garage doors or home security systems... If you can just take control of one of those small industries, you will dominate it so much faster." — Owen. (You will do this properly in Lesson 4: Choose your niche — this is your first taste of why it matters.)

Failure 4 — Overcomplicating delivery

"It's pretty simple to get good client results... Beginners will try to do everything at once and they will completely drown in it." — Owen. The fix: follow the basic step-by-step delivery process, and resist the urge to bolt on extras. "Simple wins here." — Owen. This is Failure 4 and its cure is the same instinct as the whole lesson: do the working thing, not the shiny new addition.

Failure 5 — Quitting too early (the biggest one)

"By far the biggest reason as to why most people fail is because they quit too early." — Owen. None of this is easy at the start — you are learning marketing, sales, fulfillment, and client success all at once. The antidote is to expect and endure the rough opening stretch: "be comfortable going through the ugly phase, you know, collect enough data to win and don't quit... because if you quit you'll never get there." — Owen.

🔑 The model works. Almost everyone who fails does so by breaking one of these five — and all five are choices you can pre-commit against right now.

Owen lists a few "honourable mention" failures too — technical breakage (funnels or pages not loading), rushing service-delivery areas, and "running from sales" / avoiding follow-up and confrontation on calls. They collapse into the same root: cutting corners and letting discomfort make the decision.

Key takeaways

  1. Three levers, two you'll use. More, Better, New — from $0 to $1M you pull More or Better and very rarely New.
  2. Volume is 90% of the issue. Most problems are not a missing clever change; they are not enough of the working thing.
  3. One question rules them all. "Will doing more volume solve it?" — almost always yes. If not, do it Better, then More.
  4. "More" has a gate. Only scale what is already profitable. Scaling a loss just loses faster.
  5. Signal → double down. When something works, the correct next move is to repeat it more, not to protect it by changing everything.
  6. Five avoidable failures. Emotional ad management, ignoring data, too-broad niche, overcomplicated delivery, and quitting too early — name them, pre-commit against them.

Common pitfalls

  • ❌ Chasing New at the first sign of success. You go profitable on Meta ads and immediately want to add Google, cold email, and Instagram. Do more of the working thing instead.
  • ❌ Scaling an unprofitable unit. "Just spend more" on ads that lose money burns cash faster. Confirm profitability first.
  • ❌ Refreshing Ads Manager 10× a day and killing campaigns early. Let them breathe; judge on data, not on a bad morning.
  • ❌ Setting no target metrics before launch. With no benchmark you cannot tell "below target" (fix it — Better) from "just needs more volume" (More) — so you guess.
  • ❌ Staying too broad because a big niche "has more customers." A crowded niche is harder for a beginner than a micro-niche you can dominate.
  • ❌ Quitting in the ugly phase. The rough opening is normal, not a signal the model is broken.

🛠️ Capstone Project: Your Growth-Diagnosis Card + Five-Failure Pre-Commitment

This is the main hands-on project for the lesson. It is small on purpose: two one-page artifacts you will actually keep and reach for every time you feel stuck, panicked, or tempted by something shiny. The point is to install the decision in advance, while you are calm, so the emotional version of you can't override it later.

What you will build

Two things, ideally on a single sheet (print it, or pin it in your notes app):

  1. A Growth-Diagnosis Card — a flowchart-plus-checklist you run whenever you hit a growth question. It encodes the one-question rule and the profitability gate.
  2. A Five-Failure Pre-Commitment — a short, signed-and-dated statement in which you name each of the five failure patterns and write the specific thing you will do instead.

Each piece maps directly to a lesson idea:

  • The card's top question → the "will doing more volume solve it?" rule (Part 3).
  • The card's profitability gate → the unit-economics precondition (Part 3 reality check).
  • The pre-commitment's five clauses → the five failure patterns (Part 4).

Why this is the perfect practice

Lesson idea Where you use it in the card / pledge
More / Better / New The three-branch flow at the heart of the card
"Volume is 90% of the issue" The default answer the card nudges you toward
Profitability precondition The gate you must pass before the "More" branch
Let ads breathe / act on data Pre-commitment clauses 1 and 2
Niche down Pre-commitment clause 3
Simple delivery Pre-commitment clause 4
Don't quit early Pre-commitment clause 5

Milestones (build them in order, each one works on its own)

  1. Draw the decision flow. On the top half of the page, reproduce the Part 3 flowchart in your own words: Problem → "Will more volume solve it?" → YES: do more / NO: can I do it Better? → then More → (rare) New.
  2. Add the profitability gate. Just above the "DO MORE" box, write the check: "Unit is profitable? $ in → $ out. If no → go to Better." This one line keeps you from scaling a leak.
  3. Write the five failure clauses. For each pattern, write one sentence in the form "I will not ; instead I will ." Example clause 1: "I will not kill a campaign out of emotion; I will let it spend to my pre-set data threshold before judging it."
  4. Set your objective thresholds. Where a clause needs a number (how long an ad breathes, what target metric counts as "below benchmark"), write a placeholder now — you will replace it with real numbers in Module 5. The habit of having a threshold matters more than the exact value today.
  5. Sign and date it. Physically sign the pre-commitment. This sounds silly; it is the point. A signed, dated pledge is what "calm you" hands to "panicked you" three months from now.
  6. Stretch goals. (a) Add a "New is allowed only if…" line listing the exact conditions (More and Better genuinely exhausted, plus the one caveat Owen allows: switching to cold outreach when you have no ad budget). (b) Tape the card somewhere you actually see it. (c) Re-read it at the start of every week for the next 12 weeks (your blinders period from Lesson 2).

How you will know you are done

  • ✅ The card answers a real "what should I change?" question in under 30 seconds without you thinking hard.
  • ✅ The profitability gate is visibly before the "do more" branch — you cannot reach "scale it" without passing the money check.
  • ✅ All five failure patterns appear as "I will not… instead I will…" clauses, each with a concrete replacement behaviour.
  • ✅ It is signed and dated.

💡 Keep yourself honest: the card is only useful if you consult it when you're emotional, which is exactly when you won't want to. Put it where panicked-you will physically bump into it.

Practice exercises (optional extra reps)

What these are: small, self-contained tasks, each giving focused practice on one idea. Optional and independent; the Capstone already touches all of them, so feel free to skip straight to it.

Exercise 1: Label the lever (foundational)

Take these three scenarios and label each More, Better, or New — then say which Owen would actually pick: (a) profitable ads, you want to grow; (b) ads running but cost-per-appointment is above your target; (c) you're tempted to add a cold-email channel while your ads are working. (Answers: a → More; b → Better then More; c → he'd say do More of the ads, not New.)

Exercise 2: Run the gate (intermediate)

Write out the unit economics of one activity you could scale (real or hypothetical): cost in vs. value out per result. Decide from the number alone whether you're in "More" or "Better" territory. One paragraph.

Exercise 3: Fault-diagnosis drill (advanced)

Imagine a peer messages you: "Two weeks in, my ads aren't converting, I'm thinking of switching niches." Diagnose which of the five failure patterns they're most likely committing, and write the exact reply that redirects them to More/Better without letting them quit or chase New.

  → YES  → DO MORE  (but only if the unit is PROFITABLE — the gate)
  → NO   → do it BETTER (fix the one weak metric), then MORE

  running from sales & follow-up.
