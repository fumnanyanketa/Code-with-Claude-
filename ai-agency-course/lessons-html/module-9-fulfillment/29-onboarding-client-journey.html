In one sentence

The moment a client's payment clears, a clock starts ticking on buyer's remorse — so you run one tight onboarding call that collects payment and a signed agreement, sets honest expectations, and kicks off two parallel tracks: the invisible build behind the scenes and the visible communication that makes your client feel looked after from day one.

It builds to a hands-on Capstone Project where you build your onboarding kit — an onboarding form, an agreement template, a 9-step onboarding-call outline, and a first-7-days messaging plan, all living in your CRM. Everything before the Capstone teaches the skills you will use there. If you want to see the finish line first, jump to the "Capstone Project" section, then come back.

First-principles companion

💡 The durable idea behind this lesson. Tools change; the psychology of a new customer does not. For the timeless version:

  • Never Lose a Customer Again by Joey Coleman (book). Owen's "eight pillars of retention" come straight from it. Its core claim — that the first 100 days decide whether a customer stays — is the first-principles account of why onboarding matters more than the sale.

A few plain-language basics first

This lesson uses some everyday terms. Here they are in plain words:

  • Fulfillment: everything you do after the sale to actually deliver the result the client paid for. The opposite of "the sale," which is just getting them to say yes.
  • Buyer's remorse: the sinking "did I just waste my money?" feeling that hits soon after someone buys. Owen says 80%+ of it lands in the first 24 hours.
  • Speed to lead: how fast someone contacts a new lead after it comes in. Faster is dramatically better.
  • Card on file: saving a client's card so you can charge them again later without asking each time — needed for "pay-per-result" billing.
  • E-sign: signing a contract by typing/drawing on a screen instead of on paper (think DocuSign).
  • CRM: "customer relationship management" software — the hub where contacts, calendars, messages, and automations live. Owen uses GoHighLevel (GHL).

You do not need to memorise these. Each is explained again the first time it matters.

Why this lesson matters

You spent Module 8 learning to close. But a closed client is not a happy client — yet. As Owen puts it: "the second the payment clears, the relationship begins... there should be no dead time." The gap between "yes" and their first win is where clients quietly panic and dispute the charge. This lesson gives you the one repeatable sequence that carries a nervous buyer from payment to "you're a wizard," without letting them leak out.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. Explain the client journey after payment and why dead time is dangerous.
  2. Separate the two parallel tracks — the invisible build and the visible communication — and manage both.
  3. Set up speed-to-lead expectations with the client on the call.
  4. Collect payment (with card on file) and a signed e-agreement inside your CRM.
  5. Run the 9-step onboarding call, including the all-important resell step.
  6. Write a first-7-days daily messaging plan that prevents buyer's remorse.

Prerequisites

  • Module 8: you have closed a client (or can role-play one).
  • A CRM/booking/automation setup from Module 4 (GoHighLevel or an equivalent stack).
  • A payment processor account (Stripe, Square, or similar) in your own legal name.

Part 1: The client journey — kill the dead time

Here is the whole journey Owen describes, from the client's point of view:

PAY ──▶ ONBOARDING CALL ──▶ BUILD ──▶ LAUNCH ──▶ OPTIMIZE ──▶ RETAIN
(clears) (30–45 min,        (1–3 days,  (ads go   (read data,  (months
         set up + resell)   invisible)  live)     scale wins)  & years)

The single most important rule: no dead time between "yes" and onboarding. Why? "80% or more of that buyer's remorse happens within the first 24 hours of buying," says Owen. Silence in that window lets a client second-guess what they just paid for. So the onboarding call happens right away, and wins get chased as fast as possible.

🔑 The relationship begins the instant the payment clears. Your job for the next 24 hours is to replace doubt with momentum.

Part 2: Fulfillment is not the sale (and that's good news)

Selling and fulfilling are two different jobs. "The sale is just getting them to say yes. Fulfillment is everything after." Most beginners are nervous about fulfillment because they've never seen it live. Owen's reassurance:

🔑 Fulfillment is far more repeatable than the sale. The sale changes with every prospect; fulfillment is nearly the same every time.

Because you run ads on your own pages and pixel (covered in Module 5 and Lesson 30), there's no logging into the client's Facebook, no ad-account warm-up, no permission mess. You control all of it — which is exactly why a client can be "fully live in under 30 minutes." You're not learning a hundred things; you're perfecting one system and repeating it.

Part 3: The two tracks — make the invisible work visible

When you start delivering, two things happen at once, and you must manage both:

Track What it is Time cost If you only do this...
Your track (invisible) The real build: sub-account, automations, creative, funnel, campaign ~30–60 min once dialed in Decent results, but an unhappy client
Their track (visible) The experience: updates, voice notes, proof-of-work photos, texts, calls A few minutes daily, but needs consistency A happy client, but weak results

You need both. The client cannot see your work, so they have no idea how hard you're working unless you show them. The rule from day one:

✅ Make the invisible work visible. A photo of your screen on their campaign, a 30-second voice note, a quick video breaking something down. It costs seconds and buys trust.

Part 4: Speed to lead — the make-or-break number

Once ads run, leads arrive. A lead is not a result — it means nothing until it's contacted. And the clock is brutal:

🔑 Any lead must be contacted within 5 minutes. "78% of B2B and B2C customers buy from the vendor that responds first." — Owen

Your automations text the lead first automatically, but the client's team (or the owner) needs to call fast. On the onboarding call, confirm who handles this and have them call through your CRM's number so you can actually track their speed-to-lead. Even world-class ads and communication won't save results if speed-to-lead is bad.

Remember the pipeline: leads → booked → shown → closed. Getting a company leads doesn't matter; getting them shown appointments is what matters. Every system you build exists to push people through this cycle and stop them leaking out.

Part 5: What onboarding collects, and the two documents

Before you can build anything, you need information from the client. Collect it on an onboarding form and confirm it on the call:

  • Where to send lead/appointment notifications (email + cell)
  • Service area (location + mile radius), average ticket size, crew capacity
  • Target demographic and the job types they want
  • Legal name, address, website, socials, business hours
  • Calendar access (connect live on the call)
  • Assets: photos of them on the job, past-job pictures
  • Who is in charge of contacting leads

Collecting payment + card on file

On the call, drop a payment link in the meeting chat and let them fill it out live. The one setting that matters: "save payment details for future use." That saved card is what lets you bill per result later.

Owen's billing preference: charge clients manually once a week (he does every Friday) rather than fully automating it. His reasoning is sound: "the first time that they see a bill from you that they don't recognize... that is when they start thinking, oh my god, is this guy a scam?" Manual weekly billing with a clear, expected notice keeps errors and confusion out.

⚠️ Reality check — do NOT do this one. Owen also floats a separate trick: set up a recurring $1/day Stripe link and buy it yourself twice a day to pile up "successful payments" and make your dispute ratio look "bulletproof." Skip it. That is deliberately manipulating your processor's dispute-to-purchase ratio, it almost certainly violates Stripe's terms, and a processor that catches it can freeze or close your account — taking your real clients' billing down with it. Keep your dispute ratio low the honest way: clear billing, good communication, and refunds when warranted.

Financing (Klarna / Afterpay)

The checkout link can offer financing so a client pays, say, $300/month at 0% APR. You collect the full amount up front (minus the provider's fee — e.g. ~$830 on a $1,000 collection).

💡 Nuance. Financing is fine to offer, but it is debt on your client's balance sheet — if they miss payments it dings their credit. Only steer someone toward it when it genuinely helps them afford a service that will pay for itself, not to push a bigger package they shouldn't carry.

The agreement (e-sign)

"It's very important that you have an agreement with clients to protect each other." In GoHighLevel it's just a PDF template you upload under Payments → Documents & Contracts, then map in fields (business name, full name, address, date, signatures) and send — it goes out by SMS + email for them to sign. You can auto-send it right after they submit the onboarding form.

💡 Alternatives to GHL's e-sign: PandaDoc, Dropbox Sign (formerly HelloSign), DocuSign, or Adobe Acrobat Sign. Any of them replaces the old DocuSign-only workflow.

❌ Careful with coercive clauses. Owen's contracts elsewhere include things like a "$900 charge for false reporting." Clauses like that are often unenforceable (courts dislike penalty terms), and even trying to collect one can wreck your local reputation in a tight niche where everyone talks. Write agreements that protect both sides fairly, not ones that punish.

Picking the communication channel

Choose one channel and keep it consistent — iMessage, Slack, or WhatsApp depending on your niche. Owen's take: for contractors, use something native like iMessage ("client" in the contact name), because most of them don't use Slack or WhatsApp and won't respond fast on tools they don't know. Slack is the more scalable option once you're bigger (one channel per client, invite them as a guest). Match the channel to your ideal client, not to what you personally prefer.

💡 Alternatives: Slack ↔ Discord, Microsoft Teams, WhatsApp, plain SMS via your CRM. The rule is: pick the channel your client already lives in.

Part 6: The 9-step onboarding call

One call, ~30–45 minutes, on Zoom (so they can screen-share for the calendar hookup). Make sure they filled out the onboarding form and, ideally, signed the agreement beforehand. Invite whoever contacts the leads. The call does two things at once: get them fully set up and resell them so they leave more excited than when they paid.

1. Introduce yourself.
2. Build the client profile (confirm the numbers, area, demographic).
3. Lay out the pathway to their goals   ← this is you pitching again
4. Confirm the agreement (walk through & sign if not done).
5. Set up the CRM (log them in, connect Google Calendar, confirm where leads go).
6. Show them what THEY do (speed to lead, how to work leads, pipeline mgmt).
7. RESELL  ← the most important step
8. Set next touchpoints (bi-weekly calls booked + referral program).
9. Send them off pumped (set build expectations, end on proof).

Step 7, the resell, is the heart of the call. "You sold them once, now sell them again." This time you sell the timeline and certainty so they don't panic and churn:

  • Show proof — pull up a real current client's results live on screen.
  • Paint a realistic picture — "based on your area and price point, here's what I expect."
  • Pre-load the slow-start plan — "if it starts slow, here's exactly what we'll do" (Google review campaign, new website, etc.).

By hang-up they should know the best case, the worst case, and that you've already thought through a slow start. That's what kills the "is this even working?" panic messages.

Step 8 locks in the future: book the next two bi-weekly calls now (skipping these is a top churn cause), and introduce the referral program (e.g. $500–$1K to a client who refers a company that pays).

⚠️ Under-promise, honestly. Owen's own line for step 9: "I'd be surprised if you get an appointment in the first three to five days." Say the honest, slightly-low version — then an early win thrills them. A client promised 60 leads who gets 35 is unhappy; a client told to expect 20–25 who gets 35 is a fan. Same 35 leads, opposite outcome. Never inflate on the call to feel good in the moment; you're just pre-loading disappointment.

Part 7: The first-7-days daily messaging framework

The build is invisible, so the first week is about communication, not early results. Owen: "The first seven days matters so much more than the actual early results." Here is the real sequence he ran with a client he signed on a Monday:

Day 1 (Mon): "What's up?" — open the channel.
Day 2 (Tue): photo — "finalizing the CRM."           → "Okay, great."
Day 3 (Wed): photo — "working on the ads."           → "Sounds good."
Day 4 (Thu): short VIDEO breaking down what you built → gets them excited.
Day 5 (Fri): ADS LAUNCH. "Christina called — appt confirmed,
             I'm running it myself, feedback coming."→ "No worries, fast!"
Day 6–7:     celebrate the first booking; share insights on reading leads.

The pattern: a proof-of-work photo or voice note most days, a video mid-week, then celebrate the first win the moment it lands. It's a few minutes a day, and it's the difference between a client who feels looked-after and one who feels abandoned.

✅ What to do about it: open a message thread on day one and don't let it go quiet. Consistency beats intensity — a steady drip of small updates outperforms one big report followed by silence.

Key takeaways

  1. No dead time. 80%+ of buyer's remorse hits in the first 24 hours — onboard immediately.
  2. Two tracks, both required. The invisible build gets results; the visible communication keeps the client. Make the invisible work visible.
  3. Speed to lead is the master metric. Contact within 5 minutes; ~78% buy from the first responder.
  4. One call does it all. Set up + resell in 30–45 minutes; the resell (step 7) is the most important part.
  5. Bill manually and honestly. Weekly, expected charges — never gimmick your dispute ratio.
  6. Under-promise on purpose. Expectation minus reality is where churn is born.

Common pitfalls

  • ❌ Leaving a gap after "yes." Every quiet hour is a chance for remorse and a dispute. Kick off onboarding the same day.
  • ❌ Only doing the invisible build. Great results in silence still loses clients. Show your work daily.
  • ❌ Gaming your processor with a $1 self-buy loop. It violates terms and can get your account frozen — with real clients on it. Don't.
  • ❌ Overselling on the call. "60 jobs fast" feels good today and causes churn next month. Say the honest lower number.
  • ❌ Coercive contract clauses. A "$900 false-reporting" fee is often unenforceable and poisons your reputation. Keep agreements fair.
  • ❌ Skipping the bi-weekly call booking. Not scheduling the next touchpoints is one of the biggest churn drivers.

🛠️ Capstone Project: Build your onboarding kit

This is the main hands-on project for the lesson. You'll assemble the exact four assets you need to onboard your first real client without improvising — so when someone pays, you press "go" instead of scrambling.

What you will build

A reusable onboarding kit, mostly living inside your CRM, with four pieces:

  • An onboarding form (maps to Part 5: what onboarding collects)
  • An agreement template with e-sign fields (Part 5: the agreement)
  • A 9-step onboarding-call outline you can read off live (Part 6)
  • A first-7-days messaging plan (Part 7)

Why this is the perfect practice

Lesson idea Where you use it in the kit
No dead time / journey The messaging plan starts on payment day
What onboarding collects Every field on the onboarding form
Payment + card on file Payment-link step in the call outline
Agreement (e-sign) The uploaded, field-mapped template
The 9-step call + resell The call outline, with step 7 fleshed out
Under-promise honestly Your written expectation lines

Milestones (build them in order, each one works on its own)

  1. Create the onboarding form. In your CRM, build a form/survey collecting every field from Part 5. Smallest version: name, email, cell, service area, average ticket, assets, lead-contact person.
  2. Upload and field-map the agreement. Draft a fair one-page agreement (have an AI assistant draft it, then read every line yourself). Upload it, add signature/date/business-name fields, send a test to yourself, and sign it.
  3. Wire the payment link. Create a payment link in your processor with save-card-for-future-use on, and a redirect to your onboarding form. Test it in test mode.
  4. Write the 9-step call outline. One page, one line per step. Under step 7, write the three sub-moves (show proof, paint a realistic picture, pre-load the slow-start plan) with your actual numbers.
  5. Write your first-7-days plan. A day-by-day list of what you'll send (photo, voice note, video, first-win celebration), including your honest under-promise line for day one.
  6. Choose and set up your channel. Pick iMessage/Slack/WhatsApp for your niche and note how you'll open the thread on day one.
  7. Stretch goals. Automate: onboarding-form submission auto-sends the agreement; add a referral-program blurb to your call outline; connect a calendar so the call books itself.

How you will know you are done

  • ✅ The onboarding form is live in your CRM and you submitted a real test entry.
  • ✅ The agreement template is uploaded, field-mapped, and you e-signed a test copy.
  • ✅ The 9-step call outline is written, with step 7 fully fleshed out in your own numbers.
  • ✅ The first-7-days messaging plan exists as a checklist, including one honest under-promise line.

💡 Keep yourself honest: write your expectation lines before you're on a live call. Under pressure, everyone rounds their promises up. Pre-writing the honest number is how you avoid it.

Practice exercises (optional extra reps)

What these are: small, self-contained tasks, each giving focused practice on one idea. Optional and independent; the Capstone already touches all of them, so feel free to skip straight to it.

Exercise 1: Speed-to-lead script (foundational)

Write the two sentences you'll say on the call to confirm who contacts leads and to get them calling through your CRM number. Include the 5-minute rule and the 78% stat as your reason.

Exercise 2: Rewrite an oversell (intermediate)

Take three hype promises ("20 jobs in month one," "results by tomorrow," "60 leads a month") and rewrite each as an honest under-promise you'd actually say on an onboarding call.

Exercise 3: Two-track day plan (advanced)

For a hypothetical client you signed today, write out both tracks for the next 48 hours: the invisible build tasks and the exact visible messages (with what proof-of-work you'd attach to each).
