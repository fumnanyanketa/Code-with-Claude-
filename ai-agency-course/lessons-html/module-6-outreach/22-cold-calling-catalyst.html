In one sentence

Cold calling is a value exchange you can learn like a game: fix your mindset, master four tonality moves, hold frame so the prospect feels they came to you, build urgency so they actually show up, then reverse-engineer a daily dial target, wire up a power dialer, dial a compliant block, and log every outcome — until the numbers do the arguing for you.

🎯 Where this lesson is heading. It builds to a hands-on Capstone Project where you set up a power dialer, calculate your own reverse-engineered daily dial goal, build a Do-Not-Call / compliance checklist, and complete a first block of real, compliant dials logged in a tracker. Everything before the Capstone teaches the skills you will use there. If you want to see the finish line first, jump to the "Capstone Project" section, then come back.

First-principles companion

💡 The durable idea behind this lesson. Owen's "urgency equation" is his own riff on a well-known framework. For the timeless, tool-agnostic version:

  • $100M Offers by Alex Hormozi (2021). Hormozi's value equation — value goes up with the dream outcome and perceived likelihood of achievement, and down with time delay and effort/sacrifice — is the skeleton Owen borrows. His urgency equation just re-points those same levers (cost of delay, time sensitivity, FOMO, immediate benefit) at one narrow goal: getting the prospect to show up to the meeting. Learn Hormozi's version and Owen's stops feeling like a magic formula and starts feeling obvious.

A few plain-language basics first

This lesson uses some everyday sales and telephony terms. Here they are in plain words:

  • Cold call: a phone call to a business owner who never asked you to call. Unsolicited.
  • Outbound: any outreach where you reach out first (calls, texts, email). The opposite is inbound, where they come to you (like clicking an ad).
  • Frame: the lens through which a conversation is understood — who has the authority, who needs whom. Whoever sets the frame leads the call.
  • Urgency: a felt reason to act now rather than later. Different from scarcity (limited quantity); urgency is about time.
  • Objection: a concern or hesitation a prospect raises ("what's the price?", "let me think about it").
  • Power dialer: software that queues your leads and dials them for you one after another, so you spend time talking, not typing numbers.
  • Setter / appointment setter: a person you hire to make the calls and book meetings for you, so you can step out of the dialing.
  • DNC (Do-Not-Call): government and internal lists of numbers you are legally not allowed to cold-call. Scrubbing means removing those numbers before you dial.
  • TCPA: the US Telephone Consumer Protection Act — the federal law governing calls, texts, autodialers, and calling hours.
  • ABR (appointment booking rate): the share of dials that turn into a booked meeting.

You do not need to memorise these. Each is explained again the first time it matters.

Why this lesson matters

Cold calling is the fastest way a brand-new agency with no ad budget can put a business owner on the phone this week. Owen is blunt that it is not required to scale — "there are better and easier methods like cold SMS" — but he preaches it anyway "just for the fundamentals and the learning skills that you get from calling the prospects." Tonality, frame, and urgency are the exact same muscles you will use on every sales call for the rest of your career. Master them while the stakes are low (a stranger you will never meet) and they compound into a real show rate and close rate later. As Owen frames the payoff: "if you make $10 a cold call, that's a little bit better than making $10 an hour."

⚠️ Reality check — read this before you dial anyone. Unsolicited calling is heavily regulated, and getting it wrong is expensive. In the US: the National Do-Not-Call Registry plus state DNC laws and telemarketer-registration rules, and the TCPA, which governs consent, calling-hour windows (generally 8am–9pm in the called party's local time), caller identification, and especially any automated or predictive dialing technology. A "power dialer" can cross into autodialer territory — know which mode yours uses. Outside the US it is stricter (UK GDPR/PECR, Canada's CASL). Before your first dial you must: scrub your list against federal and state DNC registries, respect calling hours, register where your state requires it, identify yourself and your company honestly on every call, and check your local law. This is not optional and this lesson does not substitute for legal advice.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. Reframe cold calling as a value exchange and a game, so rejection stops stopping you.
  2. Apply the four aspects of tonality — confidence/clarity, warmth/friendliness, energy/enthusiasm, adaptability.
  3. Hold frame so an outbound call feels like the prospect came to you.
  4. Build urgency deliberately using the four levers of the urgency equation.
  5. Decide when to ignore an objection versus handle it on a cold call.
  6. Reverse-engineer a daily dial goal from a monthly revenue target.
  7. Set up a power dialer, run and log calls, track metrics, and plan to remove yourself.
  8. Build a DNC/compliance checklist and dial a first compliant block.

Part 1: Mindset & belief shattering

Owen opens here on purpose: "the first and arguably most important topic in all of this is your mindset." Our subconscious pulls us away from pain, and rejection reads as pain — so unless you change how you see the call, you will quietly avoid dialing. His reframes:

  • Value. "Cold calling is literally just a value exchange. Like nothing more, nothing less." Two humans, one with a proposition, one with a business, seeing if they should grow together.
  • Positivity. Program your brain to feel good about calling — you genuinely can change a business owner's results. So "never hang up abruptly or prematurely." Positive calls train your mind to enjoy the next one.
  • Rejection as progress. "Every time you experience rejection, say you love it... that means I'm getting closer." More nos mathematically walk you toward the next yes.
  • Gamification. "Tally my calls, pitches, resonations, and appointments on a whiteboard." Points make reps addictive instead of draining.
  • Mirroring. "Treat every single cold call you get with respect" — how you receive calls shows up in how you make them.

🔑 Rejection is not a verdict on you; it is a coin you spend on the way to a yes. Reframe it or it eats you alive.

A critical note on the harsher "priming" tricks

Owen also passes along two tricks he heard from mentors, and it is worth being honest about them:

  • Prank-call priming — making a few prank calls first to get used to the nervousness.
  • "Nobody's real / everyone's a robot" — telling yourself the person on the other end isn't real "because they kind of like aren't... you'll never see them in your entire life," so rejection carries no weight.

These do lower fear, and lower fear means you actually dial. But notice the cost: a mindset that says the other person isn't real is one keystroke away from treating them as if they don't matter — talking over them, misleading them, not caring whether your offer actually helps. That directly contradicts Owen's own better advice (be warm, be respectful, mirror how you'd want to be treated).

💡 Nuance — keep the fear-reduction, drop the dehumanizing. Use the version that survives contact with a real human: this stranger's opinion of my pitch is low-stakes for me, so I can relax — not this person isn't real, so anything goes. Stay respectful and honest. Owen's mirroring tip is the tell: the person is real enough that how you treat them trains your own brain.

Part 2: Tonality — the four aspects

"Tonality is arguably one of the most important aspects... it's super super overlooked." Owen breaks it into four:

Aspect What it sounds like Quick fix
Confidence & clarity "We can get you five to six roof replacements completely risk-free" — not "um, we could probably get you more customers... I think it would work out." Cut filler words (um, like, you know). Speak in specifics. If you have a non-local accent, name it lightly up front rather than hiding it.
Warmth & friendliness A voice that's clearly smiling. Actually smile — people hear it. And never end on a bad note or hang up abruptly; it trains your own brain to dread the next call.
Energy & enthusiasm Varied pitch, genuine interest — not monotone, not a screaming car salesman. Aim for the middle with movement. Monotone is "so damn boring"; overhyped kills trust.
Adaptability Matching the prospect's tone — formal with the formal, casual with the casual, even matching a dropped swear word. Listen first, then mirror. Matching tone builds rapport fast.

✅ What to do about it: record a couple of your own calls and listen back. You cannot hear your filler words and flat pitch in the moment — only on replay. That single habit fixes tonality faster than any tip.

Part 3: Frame — hold the authority

Frame is "the perspective, context, or lens through which a... conversation... is presented and interpreted." Whoever sets it leads. On a cold call the frame starts against you, because you called them — so you have to flip it.

  • Set expectations early. "Hey, I appreciate you taking the time. I'm going to ask you a few questions and see if you're a good fit for this — if not, no worries." That one line makes you the person deciding fit.
  • Reframe so they came to you. "Frame it so that they are coming to you and need your services and you aren't coming to them — even though technically you are." Neediness repels; calm indifference attracts.
  • Why cold SMS frames better. With an ad, the prospect clicks and comes to you — perfect frame. With a cold call, you interrupted them. Texting first "break[s] the barrier and then they give you a call," which flips the frame in your favor. (This is why Lesson 21 pairs with this one.)

🔑 You don't need any single client. Sound like it. "I don't really care what happens on this call — I'm just offering this because the people who take it benefit a lot" is far more attractive than "please, I need a client."

Part 4: Urgency — the urgency equation

Prospects default to "this can wait." Your job is to move them to "this matters now," or they simply won't show up to the meeting they booked. Owen: "if there's no urgency... honestly there is no reason to even show up." Note urgency ≠ scarcity — it's about time, not quantity.

His urgency equation (his riff on Hormozi's value equation — see the first-principles companion):

              perceived cost of delay  ×  time sensitivity
Urgency  =  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
             perceived risk of missing out (FOMO)  ×  desire
                          for immediate benefit

Four levers you build naturally into the conversation — never recite them:

  • Perceived cost of delay — the price of waiting. "The longer you wait, the harder it'll be to catch up with competitors who are already getting leads online."
  • Time sensitivity — a real time box. "We're filling up with clients in the US as we speak, so let's get on a call in the next 24–48 hours."
  • FOMO (risk of missing out) — others already moved. "Several of your competitors have signed on and are seeing ~40 extra leads a month."
  • Desire for immediate benefit — the fast upside. "We can have your campaign live in about a week, so you'll see results almost immediately."

💡 Build it, don't read it. "You shouldn't directly say these examples in calls" unless they flow. Urgency isn't binary (yes/no) — it's a dial. The more you build, the higher your show rate. Owen: a call with no built urgency shows at ~20%; a call with it shows at 60–75%.

❌ Do not manufacture false urgency. "Filling up in the US as we speak" and "your competitors already signed" must be true. Inventing fake deadlines or phantom competitor sign-ups is a misrepresentation — it books meetings that churn and wrecks your local reputation. Urgency should surface a real cost of waiting, not fabricate one.

Part 5: Objections — usually don't handle them

This is the counter-intuitive part. On a sales call you handle objections carefully (there's a two-hour lesson on it later, in Module 8). On a cold call, mostly you don't.

Owen's logic: "if they're throwing a ton of objections at you just for hopping on a simple little Zoom call, then chances are they are not going to be the best fit." Heavy objectors show up less, close less, and churn more — so they cost you later even if you win them now.

So the move depends on your read:

  • If they feel like a good fit and toss out one objection ("how does it work?"), ignore it and ask a question instead — almost interrupting: "How long have you been in business?" You cut through the smoke and keep frame.
  • If they throw objection after objection, "just get off the call. It's not worth your time."
  • Keep a small objection vault of frame-holding responses for the in-between cases — good practice, and handy when you blank.

✅ What to do about it: a fit prospect shouldn't throw many objections to a free call. Treat a pile of objections as data — a fit signal, not a wall to break through.

Part 6: Reverse-engineer your daily dial goal

Here's where cold calling stops feeling scary and starts feeling like arithmetic. Owen walks it backward from a revenue target. Example numbers (illustrative — plug in your own):

Goal:                 $10,000 / month cash collected
÷ $2,000 per client  → 5 clients
÷ 20% close rate     → 25 showed-up meetings
÷ 50% show rate      → 50 booked meetings
÷ 3% booking rate    → ~1,666 dials / month
÷ 20 dialing days    → ~83 dials / day

Then the fun part:
$10,000 ÷ 1,666 dials → ~$6 earned per dial

Every dial is worth about six dollars whether or not that specific person says yes — because the average is what pays you. That reframes a "no" from a failure into a ~$6 coin you just earned toward the next yes.

🔑 The more inputs, the more money — it's a science. Owen's rule: figure your daily dial number, then double it to guarantee you clear the target.

Part 7: The power dialer, running calls & removing yourself

Set up the dialer

A power dialer queues leads and dials them for you, so you're talking instead of typing. Owen mentions the Wave power dialer (~$59/mo) and a built-in GoHighLevel dialer with a ready snapshot (its own pipeline plus a power dialer campaign automation that auto-moves each lead through Call 1 → Call 2 → Call 3 and into Interested / Not interested / Follow-up based on the outcome you log).

💡 Alternatives (don't get locked in). Instead of Wave or the GHL dialer: Aircall, Kixie, PhoneBurner, JustCall, Orum. For cleaning your list before you dial, Owen uses Clear Out Phone to strip landline numbers — alternatives: Twilio Lookup, NumVerify, Clearout. Pick one; they all do the same job.

⚠️ Dialer mode is a compliance decision. "Predictive" or fully automated dialing modes are exactly what the TCPA scrutinizes. Prefer a manual / click-to-dial mode where you press to place each call, keep an accurate caller ID, and never dial a number you haven't DNC-scrubbed.

The lead-to-dial flow

Scrape leads  →  Clear Out Phone (drop landlines)  →  DNC scrub
   →  master lead list (by state)  →  import to dialer/CRM
   →  add to power-dialer campaign  →  DIAL

Two rules Owen stresses: book the meeting while you're still on the call — send the calendar invite, have them accept it and watch any pre-call video before you hang up — and always book with the same phone number you called from, so the record matches and they're auto-tagged as interested.

Log every call

After each call, mark the outcome (no answer / follow-up / not interested / interested). This is what drives the automation and your metrics. Then track daily: dials, pitches, resonations (people who liked the offer), bookings, sales calls taken, sales made, money made.

❌ Don't skip the tracker. If your booking rate is low you can't fix a system you aren't measuring. "The only way to [diagnose] is to track metrics." And do a real volume — Owen: "don't ever do 34 dials any day. Do like 100 at least."

Remove yourself

Once the system works, the goal is to stop being the one dialing. "When looking to remove yourself from the entire system, which I recommend doing sooner than later" — hire and train setters to run the SOP, and you step up to closing and fulfillment. A logged, reverse-engineered system is exactly what makes a setter's job teachable: they inherit the dial target, the script, and the tracker.

💡 Scripts are illustrative. Owen keeps his script "pretty bare bones... so you guys can iterate on it" using the frame/urgency/tonality skills above. Treat any script, price, or KPI here as an example to adapt, not gospel.

Key takeaways

  1. Mindset first. Cold calling is a value exchange and a game; rejection is progress, not a verdict.
  2. Keep the useful reframe, drop the dehumanizing one. "Low stakes" beats "they're not real." Stay respectful and honest.
  3. Tonality is 4 dials: confidence/clarity, warmth/friendliness, energy/enthusiasm, adaptability. Record and replay yourself.
  4. Frame flips the call so they feel they came to you; non-neediness is magnetic.
  5. Urgency is a dial you build, using cost of delay × time sensitivity ÷ FOMO ÷ immediate benefit — naturally, and only when true.
  6. On cold calls, mostly ignore objections — a fit prospect throws few; heavy objectors are a churn signal.
  7. The math is friendly: ~$6/dial means every no pays. Set a daily number, double it.
  8. Compliance is the price of entry: DNC-scrub, respect calling hours, identify yourself, know your dialer's mode.

Common pitfalls

  • ❌ Dialing before scrubbing DNC lists or checking calling hours. This is a legal risk, not a style choice. Scrub, register where required, and dial only 8am–9pm the called party's local time.
  • ❌ Taking "everyone's a robot" literally. It dehumanizes the person and leaks into how you treat them. Use the low-stakes reframe instead.
  • ❌ Reciting the urgency levers. Read aloud, they sound manipulative. Weave them in, and only when they're true.
  • ❌ Trying to overcome a wall of objections on a cold call. You're winning a bad-fit client who'll churn. Ask a question or hang up.
  • ❌ Not logging calls / dialing tiny volume. 34 dials tells you nothing. Do 100+, log every outcome, let the metrics talk.
  • ❌ Sounding needy. "I really need a client" repels. Calm indifference books meetings.

🛠️ Capstone Project: your dialing engine, live

This is the main hands-on project for the lesson. You'll prove you can turn a revenue goal into a daily number, set up the tooling, get compliant, and actually dial — small on purpose, but fully real.

What you will build

A working cold-call engine: a power dialer loaded with a scrubbed, compliant lead list, a reverse-engineered daily dial target written down, a DNC/compliance checklist you've actually completed, and a filled-in tracker showing your very first block of real dials.

  • Dialer + snapshot → Part 7
  • Reverse-engineered daily target → Part 6
  • DNC/compliance checklist → the Reality-check callout + Part 7
  • First logged dial block → Parts 1–5 in action

Milestones (build them in order, each one works on its own)

  1. Set your dial target. Plug your real numbers into the Part 6 chain (revenue → price → close rate → show rate → booking rate → dials/day). Double it. Write the daily number down.
  2. Set up the power dialer. Create your dialer account (Wave, GHL, or an alternative) and confirm it can place a manual/click-to-dial call. Import a small test lead.
  3. Build & complete a compliance checklist. Federal + state DNC scrub done · calling hours confirmed for your leads' time zone · state registration checked · your name + company identification line written · dialer mode confirmed manual. Tick every box.
  4. Load a scrubbed list. Run leads through Clear Out Phone (or an alternative) to drop landlines, DNC-scrub, then import and add to the dialer campaign.
  5. Dial a first compliant block. Do a real session — aim for a meaningful count (Owen says 100+; start smaller if you must, but make it real and compliant). Smile, hold frame, build a little urgency, book anyone interested on the call with your calendar link and the same number you called from.
  6. Log everything. Record dials, pitches, resonations, bookings in the tracker for the session.
  7. Stretch goals. Record and review one call for tonality; write three objection-vault responses; draft the one-page SOP a future setter would follow to run this exact system.

How you will know you are done

  • ✅ The dialer places a call and moves leads through the campaign as expected.
  • ✅ A reverse-engineered daily dial target is written down (and doubled).
  • ✅ Every box on the DNC/compliance checklist is ticked before you dialed.
  • ✅ A first real, compliant dial session is fully logged in the tracker.

💡 Keep yourself honest: the checklist comes before the first dial, not after. If you can't tick "DNC-scrubbed" and "calling hours confirmed," you are not ready to press dial — full stop.

Practice exercises (optional extra reps)

What these are: small, self-contained tasks, each giving focused practice on one idea. Optional and independent; the Capstone already touches all of them, so feel free to skip straight to it.

Exercise 1: Tonality replay (foundational)

Record yourself reading your opener three ways — flat, car-salesman, and "smiling middle." Listen back. Circle every filler word. Notice which one you'd book a meeting from.

Exercise 2: Urgency drill (intermediate)

Write one natural sentence for each of the four urgency levers, tailored to your niche. Then rewrite them so they'd survive a prospect saying "prove it" — i.e., only claims that are actually true.

Exercise 3: Frame flip (advanced)

Script the first 20 seconds of a cold call twice: once needy ("I'd love to work with you"), once frame-holding ("I ask a few questions to see if you're even a fit"). Read both aloud. Do a mock call with a friend using the second.
